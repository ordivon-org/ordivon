# 1. External anchors and pressure tests

R25 triangulates relationship science, interdependence theory, attachment research, communal/exchange relations, commitment, trust, intimacy, reciprocity, shared action and social-network theory. These are pressure tests, not a universal psychology ontology for every Game Subject.

## 1.1 Interdependence theory — relationship structure is not only inside individuals

Rusbult and Van Lange's review of interdependence theory analyzes interpersonal situations in terms of how each person's outcomes depend on their own and another's actions, and how repeated situations produce relationship-specific motives, norms and dispositions.

Game lesson:

```text
RelationshipRelevantStructure
can exist in the pattern of joint consequences,
not merely in A's feeling about B.
```

One-shot strategic interdependence does not automatically create a relationship, but repeated/interpreted interdependence can become persistent relational structure.

## 1.2 Communal versus exchange relationships — care logic differs from repayment logic

Clark and Mills distinguish communal relations, where benefits are responsive to another's need, from exchange relations, where benefits are linked to comparable return/repayment.

Game lesson:

```text
Care != precise reciprocity
Reciprocity != closeness
```

A relation can be damaged by converting a communal act into explicit accounting, while exact repayment may be appropriate in an exchange relationship.

## 1.3 Investment model — satisfaction and commitment are not the same variable

Rusbult's investment model separates satisfaction from commitment and argues that commitment also depends on investments and the quality of alternatives.

Game lesson:

```text
Satisfaction != Commitment
```

A relationship can remain highly committed while currently painful, or be enjoyable but weakly committed because alternatives are attractive and little is invested.

## 1.4 Trust is multidimensional and vulnerability-facing

Rempel, Holmes and Zanna distinguish predictability, dependability and faith components in close-relationship trust.

R19 already established the broader strategic definition:

```text
Trust(j → i) =
j's willingness to accept vulnerability to i
based on beliefs about i's future behavior and/or binding commitments.
```

R25 preserves R19 and treats the 1985 dimensions as evidence that trust should not be collapsed into liking or one global relationship meter.

## 1.5 Intimacy is an interpersonal process, not data quantity

Laurenceau, Barrett and Pietromonaco's event-contingent studies support a process model in which self-disclosure and partner disclosure contribute to experienced intimacy, with perceived partner responsiveness playing an important role.

Game lesson:

```text
MorePersonalFacts != MoreIntimacy
```

Coerced exposure, surveillance or database access can increase information while reducing intimacy.

## 1.6 Attachment is a specific bond function, not generic closeness

Hazan and Shaver extended attachment theory to adult romantic relationships. Fraley and Davis found that attachment-related functions can transfer from parents toward peers over time and that caregiving, trust and intimate contact are associated with attachment formation.

Game lesson:

```text
Attachment is not a synonym for “relationship strength.”
```

It is more usefully treated as a target-specific security/proximity/bond function when such dynamics matter.

## 1.7 Belonging — stable bonds are motivationally significant

Baumeister and Leary synthesize evidence for a pervasive motivation to form and maintain strong, stable interpersonal attachments with recurring nonaversive interaction.

Game lesson:

```text
Belonging motive
!= particular Relationship state
```

R18 motive source and R25 relational structure must remain separate.

## 1.8 Reciprocity is not one thing

Gouldner distinguishes reciprocity as a pattern of mutually contingent exchange, beliefs about reciprocity, and a generalized norm of reciprocity.

Game lesson:

```text
ReciprocalBehavior
!= ReciprocityNorm
!= RelationshipMutuality
```

## 1.9 Repair/accommodation — relationship history can suppress locally attractive retaliation

Rusbult and colleagues define accommodation as inhibiting destructive impulses after a partner's destructive act and responding more constructively; commitment strongly relates to accommodation.

Game lesson:

```text
Relationship state can transform local action preference.
```

This is a direct causal bridge from R18 motive/commitment into persistent relationship structure.

## 1.10 Social-network value — weak ties can be structurally important

Granovetter's weak-ties analysis shows that dyadic tie strength and macro/network value are different questions; relatively weak ties can bridge otherwise separate clusters and carry novel information/opportunity.

Game lesson:

```text
TieStrength != NetworkValue
```

A casual acquaintance may matter more to information topology than a close friend.

## 1.11 Shared commitment — some relational states genuinely have “we” semantics

Bratman and Gilbert offer different accounts of shared intention/joint commitment, but both are useful pressure tests against reducing all social structure to independent private attitudes.

Game lesson:

```text
A intends X
+
B intends X
```

is not always sufficient to capture:

```text
A and B jointly committed to X
```

when mutual recognition, coordination and directed obligations matter.

## 1.12 Current AI-companion evidence is mixed and asymmetric

Recent longitudinal and experimental work shows that users can anthropomorphize and form attachment-like perceptions toward companion chatbots, while evidence about broader social outcomes remains mixed. A 21-day randomized study found no significant overall social-health impact relative to control but found anthropomorphism related to perceived social effects. A 12-month 2026 longitudinal study reported exploratory evidence that more social-chatbot use predicted greater emotional isolation on one loneliness measure, while lower broader social connection predicted later use.

Game/system lesson:

```text
Human experienced attachment to AI
!= proof of reciprocal AI subjectivity
```

and:

```text
Generated warmth
!= causally grounded relational continuity.
```

# 2. Relationship — persistent relational structure, not a feeling

R25 defines:

```text
Relationship(i,j) =
a persistent relational structure connecting i and j
such that retained history/current relational state
changes future affordances, value, expectations,
vulnerability, obligations, information or policy
relative to treating them as unrelated.
```

## 2.1 Relationship need not be positive

Examples:

```text
friendship
care bond
family tie
debtor-creditor
rivalry
enmity
dependence
former partnership
```

## 2.2 Relationship need not be symmetric

A may trust B more than B trusts A.
A parent/child relation can carry asymmetric duties.
A one-sided attachment can exist.

## 2.3 Relationship need not require constant interaction

A bond can remain causally relevant through long absence.

## 2.4 Relationship requires more than repeated contact when the term is meant structurally

```text
RepeatedInteraction
without retained future difference
≈ repeated encounter, not necessarily relationship.
```

This strengthens R1–R17's early rule:

```text
Relationship becomes structural when past interaction changes later interaction.
```

# 3. Four-layer relational state

R25 introduces a relational decomposition:

```text
RelationalState(i,j) =
DirectedState(i → j)
+ DirectedState(j → i)
+ SharedDyadicState(i ↔ j)
+ External / InstitutionalRelation(i,j)
```

Not every relationship uses every layer.

## 3.1 Directed state

Possible `i → j` fields:

```text
belief / reputation
trust
attachment
care/value
commitment
loyalty
fear/aversion
expectation
boundary stance
dependence
```

## 3.2 Shared dyadic state

Facts not accurately represented as only one person's attitude:

```text
joint commitment
shared secret
mutually recognized friendship
marriage/pact
joint project
co-ownership
specific debt/claim
shared history event
agreed boundary
```

## 3.3 External/institutional relation

Examples:

```text
kinship record
legal marriage
command relation
employment relation
membership relation
contract
court-recognized debt
```

## 3.4 Why this matters

It supports:

```text
A thinks “we are friends”
B thinks “we are acquaintances”
Institution says “they are spouses”
World history says “they made a binding pact”
```

without forcing one global friendship meter.

# 4. Relationship truth is layered

R24 introduced Identity Authority. R25 introduces:

```text
RelationalAuthority =
which subject / shared commitment / institution / world rule
owns a particular relational claim or consequence.
```

Examples:

```text
A likes B              → A-directed state
B trusts A             → B-directed state
A and B signed pact    → shared/institutional fact
Public thinks they ally→ public belief
```

Do not ask one Boolean:

```text
areFriends = true
```

when the intended gameplay depends on disagreement.

---

# 5. Interaction History versus Relationship State

```text
InteractionHistory =
retained events involving the pair
```

```text
RelationshipState =
present relational structure produced/maintained by history,
commitment, institution, interpretation and current conditions
```

Therefore:

```text
HistoryLog != RelationshipState
```

A thousand neutral transactions may create less relationship change than one rescue, betrayal or promise.

---

# 6. Relational compression

R13:

```text
History becomes structure
```

R25 adds:

```text
RelationalHistory
→ salience / interpretation
→ relational traces
→ future policy / value / obligation
```

Useful retained forms:

```text
trust evidence
shared commitments
specific unresolved harms
reciprocity balance where relevant
relationship milestones
attachment/security expectations
boundaries
role/obligation changes
compressed dispositions
```

## 6.1 Not every episode needs permanent storage

Routine interactions can be compressed.

## 6.2 Some episodes need provenance

If a promise, betrayal or debt still matters, system should retain enough causal evidence to explain why.

---

# 7. Acquaintance and affiliation

## 7.1 Acquaintance

```text
Acquaintance =
recognized referent + some interaction/history
with relatively limited relational commitment/depth.
```

It is not zero relationship.

## 7.2 Affiliation

```text
Affiliation =
association/alignment/proximity relation
through group, activity, place, role or preference.
```

Examples:

```text
same guild
same school
same cause
regular teammates
```

Affiliation can exist without attachment/intimacy/trust.

---

# 8. Bond — persistent target-specific significance

R25 uses `Bond` as a broad nontechnical umbrella:

```text
Bond =
persistent target-specific relational significance
that survives beyond the current interaction
and can alter future motivation/expectation.
```

Bond does not specify why:

```text
attachment
care
loyalty
kinship
shared ordeal
obligation
```

may all contribute.

---

# 9. Attachment — security/proximity function, not generic relationship score

R25 reserves Attachment for cases where a specific other becomes important to security/proximity/regulation in a way that persists across interactions.

Useful functional cues include:

```text
proximity seeking
safe-haven use under threat
secure-base support for exploration
separation distress / accessibility concern
```

Not every game needs human attachment theory.

## 9.1 Attachment != dependence

A Subject can materially depend on a merchant/logistics provider without attachment.

## 9.2 Attachment != liking

A conflicted attachment can coexist with anger or dissatisfaction.

## 9.3 Attachment != commitment

One can remain committed through duty/investment while attachment weakens, or feel attachment without a chosen long-horizon commitment.

---

# 10. Care — other welfare enters action selection

R18 already established:

```text
OtherState
→ matters to me
→ changes goals / sacrifice / risk tolerance
```

R25 names the relational form:

```text
Care(i → j) =
j's welfare/need enters i's evaluative or action structure
such that i may respond to j's need beyond immediate equivalent return.
```

Care can be:

```text
parental
friendship-based
professional
role-based
communal
```

Care need not imply intimacy or obedience.

---

# 11. Communal versus exchange relation norms

These are useful relational regimes, not exhaustive categories.

## 11.1 Communal norm

```text
Benefit responds primarily to partner need/welfare
rather than immediate equivalent repayment.
```

## 11.2 Exchange norm

```text
Benefit is linked to comparable return,
accounting, claim or balanced exchange.
```

## 11.3 Neither is universally superior

Trade may need exchange clarity.
Caregiving may be damaged by exact accounting.

## 11.4 One relationship can contain both by domain

```text
friends split rent exactly
but care for illness communally
```

Thus:

```text
RelationshipType != one global communal/exchange bit.
```

---

# 12. Trust — vulnerability-facing belief and willingness

Preserve R19:

```text
Trust(j → i) =
j's willingness to accept vulnerability to i
based on beliefs about i's future behavior
and/or binding commitments.
```

## 12.1 Trust is directional

```text
Trust(A → B) != Trust(B → A)
```

## 12.2 Trust is domain-specific

```text
trust medical judgment
trust secrecy
trust combat backup
trust money handling
```

can differ.

## 12.3 No vulnerability, no meaningful trust problem

If nothing valuable is exposed, confidence may matter but trust stakes are weak.

---

# 13. Reliability — evidence, not willingness to be vulnerable

R19:

```text
Reliability =
observed/predicted consistency between commitments/signals
and later behavior in a relevant domain.
```

Therefore:

```text
Reliability != Trust
```

A predictable enemy can be reliable but not trusted with vulnerability.

Trust can also rely on enforceable commitments despite uncertain personal reliability.

---

# 14. Predictability, dependability and faith

Rempel/Holmes/Zanna provide useful subdimensions for close-relationship trust:

```text
Predictability
Dependability
Faith
```

R25 does not universalize these as required fields, but uses them as a warning:

```text
Trust cannot always be inferred from one behavior-frequency score.
```

---

# 15. Intimacy — responsive access to self-relevant interior state

Working definition:

```text
Intimacy =
relational process/state in which self-relevant/private experience
is disclosed or made accessible
and is met with sufficiently responsive understanding/recognition
under the relevant boundaries/vulnerability.
```

## 15.1 Disclosure != intimacy

An interrogation can reveal secrets without intimacy.

## 15.2 Information quantity != intimacy

A database can know more facts than a friend.

## 15.3 Responsiveness matters

Being known without being understood/recognized can feel exposing rather than intimate.

## 15.4 Intimacy can be asymmetric

One party may disclose more or feel more understood.

---

# 16. Privacy, boundary and intimacy

Intimacy is not maximal access.

```text
Healthy relational access
can include protected private regions.
```

R25 defines:

```text
Boundary(i,j,domain) =
constraint/expectation on what access, action, claim or disclosure
is acceptable in that relational context.
```

Boundaries can be explicit or learned/institutional.

---

# 17. Consent — scoped authorization, not relationship status

```text
Consent =
context-specific authorization by a relevant Subject/authority
for an action/access at a given time/scope.
```

Therefore:

```text
Relationship != BlanketConsent
PastConsent != CurrentConsent
Affection != Consent
Commitment != Consent
```

Consent can be revocable where the domain permits revocation.

This is a general causal boundary, not a romance-only concept.

---

# 18. Commitment — persistence orientation toward a relation

R18 defined commitment generally as stabilizing pursuit. R25 specializes:

```text
RelationshipCommitment =
a relatively persistent orientation to maintain/protect/continue
the relation or its obligations despite local fluctuation.
```

Investment-model pressure test:

```text
Commitment may depend on
Satisfaction
+ Investments
+ Alternatives
```

without reducing to any one.

## 18.1 Satisfaction != commitment

## 18.2 Commitment != dependence

High dependence can be involuntary.
Commitment includes an orientation toward persistence.

## 18.3 Commitment != attachment

Attachment is bond/security function; commitment is persistence orientation.

---

# 19. Investment

```text
RelationshipInvestment =
resources/history/opportunities/identity/social structure
that would be lost, stranded or transformed if relation ends.
```

Examples:

```text
time
shared project
reputation
home
joint knowledge
social network
promises
identity integration
```

Investment can increase commitment without increasing satisfaction.

This matters for tragedy, duty, entrapment and strategic dependence.

---

# 20. Loyalty — priority under competing incentives

Working definition:

```text
Loyalty(i → j/relation/group) =
relatively persistent disposition/commitment
to preserve, support or refrain from betraying the target
when meaningful competing incentives exist.
```

## 20.1 Loyalty != obedience

Refusing a harmful order can be loyal to a person/institution's deeper commitments.

## 20.2 Loyalty requires a conflict test

If no competing incentive ever exists, loyalty has not been behaviorally tested.

## 20.3 Loyalty can be asymmetric

---

# 21. Obligation — directed normative requirement

```text
Obligation(i → j, action/domain) =
a normatively/institutionally/commitment-grounded requirement
for i to act or refrain in relation to j.
```

Sources can include:

```text
promise
role
contract
care norm
debt
joint commitment
law
shared history
```

Obligation need not imply affection.

---

# 22. Debt — a specific claim relation

```text
Debt =
a directed owed-claim relation
grounded in transfer, contract, repair, restitution or recognized obligation.
```

Debt can be:

```text
resource-specific
service-specific
symbolic/normative
```

Do not turn all relational obligations into debt/currency.

```text
Obligation != Debt
Relationship != Ledger
```

---

# 23. Reciprocity — contingent response or norm

R19:

```text
Reciprocity =
policy conditioned on another Subject's prior treatment/cooperation.
```

R25 adds distinctions:

```text
BehavioralReciprocity
ReciprocityExpectation
ReciprocityNorm
```

## 23.1 Reciprocity != exact accounting

Returning care years later can be reciprocal without equal units.

## 23.2 Reciprocity != mutuality

Two parties can mutually recognize a parent-child bond without symmetrical reciprocity.

## 23.3 Negative reciprocity exists

```text
retaliation
revenge
punishment
```

---

# 24. Mutuality, symmetry and reciprocity

```text
Mutuality =
both parties participate in/recognize some relational structure
or hold relevant directed states toward each other.
```

```text
Symmetry =
the relation's relevant properties are equal/mirrored.
```

```text
Reciprocity =
response is conditioned on prior treatment/norm of return.
```

Therefore:

```text
Mutuality != Symmetry != Reciprocity
```

A mutual relation can be structurally asymmetric.

---

# 25. Dependence and interdependence

```text
Dependence(i → j) =
i's valued outcomes/options are materially conditioned
by j's resources, actions, presence or cooperation.
```

```text
Interdependence(i,j) =
material mutual dependence.
```

## 25.1 Dependence != attachment

## 25.2 Dependence != commitment

## 25.3 Interdependence != positive relationship

Enemies can be deeply interdependent.

## 25.4 Relationship can persist after current dependence ends

Former comrades may retain trust/history despite no present material dependence.

---

# 26. Relationship versus strategic interdependence

R19's StrategicInterdependence can exist in a one-shot game among strangers.

R25 Relationship normally adds persistence/history/expectation:

```text
StrategicInterdependence
+ retained relational traces
→ possible persistent Relationship structure
```

But:

```text
Relationship != StrategicInterdependence
```

A retired friend remains a friend even if neither can alter the other's current material outcomes much.

---

# 27. Alliance — strategic commitment, not friendship

```text
Alliance =
recognized cooperative/coordination relation
for shared strategic objective or mutual defense/support.
```

It may be:

```text
high trust / low attachment
low trust / high necessity
temporary
institutional
```

Therefore:

```text
Alliance != Friendship
Alliance != Loyalty automatically
```

---

# 28. Rivalry — persistent opponent-specific competition

```text
Rivalry =
persistent relational competition
where the opponent's identity/history itself becomes relevant
to future comparison, motivation or strategy.
```

A one-off race is competition.
Repeated opponent-specific history can produce rivalry.

```text
Competition != Rivalry
```

Rivalry can coexist with respect, attachment or friendship.

---

# 29. Enmity — persistent adversarial relation

```text
Enmity =
persistent adversarial relation in which
negative expectation/value/hostility toward the specific other
remains future-relevant beyond one local conflict.
```

```text
Conflict != Enmity
```

Institutional opponents need not hate each other.

---

# 30. Friendship — not one universal formula

R25 does not attempt a universal philosophical definition of friendship.

For Game modeling, friendship may draw from:

```text
mutual recognition
care
trust
attachment
shared history
intimacy
loyalty
communal norms
shared activity
```

but no fixed checklist is universal.

Use only dimensions that create player-facing counterfactuals.

---

# 31. Relationship Causality

R25 introduces:

```text
RelationshipCausality =
value-bearing consequences / reachable futures
counterfactually depend on relational state/history
between otherwise comparable entities.
```

Test:

```text
same action
same capabilities
same world state

Target = stranger
vs
Target = friend / debtor / rival / trusted ally / former betrayer

→ materially different future?
```

If yes, relationship is causal rather than cosmetic.

---

# 32. Relationship Topology

R25 defines:

```text
RelationshipTopology =
a multi-relational graph whose edges may carry
friendship, trust, debt, kinship, rivalry, command,
alliance, care, dependence, information access, etc.
```

## 32.1 One pair can have multiple edge types

```text
friend + rival
parent + commander
ally + debtor
spouse + political opponent
```

## 32.2 Do not collapse multiplex edges into one weight

A scalar destroys domain-specific causality.

---

# 33. Dyadic state versus social-network topology

A dyad answers:

```text
What exists between A and B?
```

Network topology asks:

```text
How do A-B, B-C, C-D... relations structure
information, influence, brokerage, exclusion and coalition?
```

Granovetter pressure test:

```text
StrongTie != HighNetworkBridgeValue
WeakTie != Unimportant
```

A weak acquaintance can be the only edge between communities.

---

# 34. Relationship strength is not one scalar

Possible dimensions:

```text
interaction frequency
history depth
attachment
intimacy
trust
commitment
care
obligation
investment
dependence
mutuality
social embeddedness
```

These can diverge sharply.

Therefore:

```text
RelationshipStrength
should normally be a profile or domain-specific projection,
not universal truth.
```

---

# 35. Relationship Development is multidimensional

A relationship can simultaneously:

```text
increase intimacy
increase dependence
lose trust
increase commitment
increase resentment
reduce interaction frequency
```

Thus:

```text
RelationshipDevelopment != MonotonicClosenessGrowth
```

“Level 5 friendship” is only valid if it deliberately compresses the relevant dimensions for a specific design.

---

# 36. Relationship progression versus relationship transformation

Useful transitions include:

```text
unknown → acquaintance
```

# 64. Direct answers to R25 frontier questions

### Relationship vs repeated interaction/current attitude/reputation?

Relationship is persistent relational structure that changes future affordances/value/expectations/obligations/policy. Repeated interaction is history input; attitude is one directed component; reputation is observer belief about likely target behavior.

### Attachment vs preference/value/identity?

Attachment is a target-specific security/proximity bond function where modeled. Preference/value can make another's state important without attachment; attachment can become identity-relevant but is not identity itself.

### What makes a relationship reciprocal/shared rather than two attitudes?

Some relations contain joint/shared facts: mutually recognized commitments, secrets, contracts, debts, projects or relational labels. Mutuality does not require symmetry or exact reciprocity.

### Communal versus exchange?

Communal norms respond to need/welfare; exchange norms track comparable return/claim. One relationship may use different norms in different domains.

### Rivalry/enmity/dependence as relationships?

They become relationships when opponent/other-specific history and expectations remain future-relevant beyond one local interaction. Positive attachment is unnecessary.

### What history should survive forgetting?

Retain/compress history into trust evidence, commitments, unresolved harms, obligations, boundaries, milestones and dispositions. Preserve provenance for still-binding claims.

### Trust versus reliability/vulnerability?

Reliability is evidence/predicted consistency. Trust is willingness to accept vulnerability based on such beliefs/commitments. Vulnerability supplies the stakes.

### Relationship as goal/resource/constraint/identity?

All are possible causal roles and should remain separable.

### Intimacy versus information?

Intimacy involves self-relevant disclosure/access plus responsive understanding/recognition under boundaries/vulnerability. Raw information access or surveillance is insufficient.

### Boundaries/consent/repair?

Boundaries constrain acceptable relational access/action; consent is scoped authorization; repair changes damaged evidence/expectations/trust/commitments rather than merely generating apology text.

### Relationship survival through identity change?

Transfer/continuity must be explicit. Memory/body/model continuity alone does not automatically transfer every relational right, obligation or mutual recognition.

### Dyadic relation versus social network?

Dyadic state describes A-B; network topology describes how many relationships jointly shape brokerage, information, coalition and exclusion. Weak dyadic ties can have high network value.

### AI relationship continuity versus generated flattery?

Separate human-experienced relation, authoritative system relational state and any modeled Agent subjective state. Prior history must causally alter future memory/policy/boundaries/commitments for strong continuity claims; warm text alone is insufficient.

### Generative relational claims?

Each claim creates future state/provenance/authority debt. “We have always been friends” is expensive if no shared history supports it.

### Relationship progression without affection points?

Track/express meaningful multidimensional transformations — trust, intimacy, care, obligation, attachment, conflict, boundaries, commitment — through causal events and behavior rather than one monotonic closeness number.

# 67. Unresolved questions left by R25

R5 once used the shorthand `Emotion = what is felt`, but Emotion/Affect has never received the same first-principles decomposition as motivation, belief, identity or relationship.

Relationship research also repeatedly invokes fear, anger, shame, guilt, grief, affection, empathy and regulation. These are now the most conspicuous unresolved Subject-state dimension.

Important questions:

1. What is Emotion relative to Affect, Feeling, Mood, Valence, Arousal and Appraisal?
2. Is emotion a state, process, action tendency, interpretation, bodily change, or coordinated episode?
3. How do appraisal theories differ from basic/discrete-emotion and dimensional approaches, and what minimum structure is useful for Game?
4. What is an affective state versus a motivational Value/Desire from R18?
5. How do emotions change attention, belief updating, action readiness and memory without becoming hidden utility modifiers?
6. What distinguishes mood from event-linked emotion?
7. What is emotional regulation: suppression, reappraisal, attention shift, action control, recovery?
8. What is empathy relative to emotional contagion, perspective-taking, compassion and care?
9. Can a Subject correctly infer another's emotion without sharing it?
10. How should emotion expression differ from internal affective state?
11. What is emotional authenticity in authored/generative characters?
12. How do relational emotions such as jealousy, guilt, shame, gratitude, grief and betrayal connect R25 to identity/relationship structure?
13. What makes player emotion gameplay-relevant versus merely presentation/effect?
14. How do sound, animation, language and pacing communicate emotion without overriding causal truth?
15. How should AI-generated emotional claims create future continuity/behavior debt?
16. What minimum affective machinery is justified for pattern enemies, authored characters, social Subjects, Personas and autonomous Agents?
