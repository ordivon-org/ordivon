# Ordivon Game Foundations Research — R28 Culture, Convention, Tradition, Ritual, Symbol, Shared Meaning, Legitimacy and Cultural Change

## 0. Status and boundary

R27 asked:

> How does experience persistently change an individual Subject?

R28 asks:

> How do practices, expectations, meanings, symbols and memories become distributed, transmitted, reproduced and transformed across Subjects and generations — and when does that structure become Culture rather than merely repeated behavior, lore or common implementation?

# 1. External pressure tests

R28 triangulates game theory, cultural evolution, cultural learning, experimental semiotics, ritual research, legitimacy research and collective-memory theory. These are pressure tests, not a mandate to import one anthropology or sociology theory wholesale.

## 1.1 Young — conventions can emerge from repeated coordination and bounded historical learning

Young's 1993 model shows how repeated coordination among changing players with incomplete information about past behavior can converge toward a stochastically stable convention.

Game lesson:

```text
Convention does not require
central rule author
or moral norm
or full common knowledge.
```

History + expectations can select one compatible solution among several.

## 1.2 Cultural transmission can generate persistent between-group differences

Henrich & Boyd's conformist-transmission models show how frequency-sensitive social transmission can help stabilize different learned behavioral patterns across groups. Their later norm-enforcement model shows that payoff-biased and conformist transmission can interact with punishment/cooperation dynamics.

Game lesson:

```text
Group difference can be culturally reproduced
without being encoded as biological/faction essence.
```

## 1.3 Cultural diffusion is not just independent optimization

Henrich's diffusion analysis distinguishes individual environmental learning from biased social transmission and shows that population adoption dynamics can bear signatures of social transmission.

Game lesson:

```text
Same optimal behavior appearing in many Subjects
!= evidence of Culture by itself.

Transmission provenance matters.
```

## 1.4 Shared intentionality supports collaborative and instructed learning, but is not Culture itself

Tomasello & Carpenter distinguish individual social-cognitive skills from transformations involving joint attention, cooperative communication, collaboration and instructed learning.

Game lesson:

```text
SharedIntentionality
can support cultural transmission / joint meaning,
but Culture can persist without every current participant
holding one active joint intention.
```

## 1.5 Iterated learning can create structure without a central designer

Kirby, Cornish & Smith's laboratory transmission chains show that artificial languages can become more structured and learnable through iterated cultural transmission.

Game lesson:

```text
Transmission can transform what is transmitted.
CulturalPersistence != exact copying.
```

## 1.6 Cumulative culture is more than accumulating more objects

Caldwell & Millen experimentally model cumulative cultural improvement across replacement generations. Tennie, Call & Tomasello frame cumulative culture in terms of preservation of modifications across generations — the ratchet problem.

Game lesson:

```text
CumulativeCulture =
later possibilities depend on retained prior modifications
that can themselves be built upon.

CumulativeCulture != more content over time.
```

## 1.7 Experimental semiotics — shared signs can emerge through interaction

Galantucci's experimental-semiotics programme studies communication systems that participants create during joint action. Misyak, Noguchi & Chater show that minimal signals can support rapidly created and context-sensitive conventions under joint inference.

Game lesson:

```text
SignalForm does not contain its cultural meaning intrinsically.
Meaning can arise from interaction + common ground + convention.
```

## 1.8 Ritual components can have causal social effects

Reddish, Fischer & Bulbulia experimentally find that synchrony combined with shared intentionality can increase cooperation. Jackson and colleagues manipulate synchrony and physiological arousal in large naturalistic groups and report effects on clustering/cooperation.

Game lesson:

```text
Ritual need not be mechanically inert flavor.
Its patterned performance can alter relation/group state.
```

But synchrony, arousal or repetition alone is not a definition of ritual.

## 1.9 Legitimacy is not reducible to coercion or formal legality

Tyler's procedural-justice research separates deterrence/instrumental compliance from normative legitimacy and finds that perceived procedural fairness can affect deference/cooperation with authorities. Suchman's organizational-legitimacy framework further separates pragmatic, moral and cognitive forms of legitimacy.

Game lesson:

```text
Same formal authority / coercive power
+ different legitimacy
→ different voluntary compliance / contestation / cooperation.
```

## 1.10 Collective memory is socially framed, selective and transmitted

Halbwachs' collective-memory tradition treats remembering as socially framed and distinguishes living collective memory from historical event inventories. Bartlett's serial-reproduction work demonstrates how stories/forms can transform as they pass through successive social reproduction.

Game lesson:

```text
CollectiveMemory != perfect shared replay of History.
```

# 2. First disambiguation — Cultural Item versus Cultural System

Broad cultural-evolution definitions often treat any socially learned information capable of affecting behavior as culture. This is useful for population modeling, but too permissive for Game ontology if we want to distinguish one transmitted rumor from a robust cultural system.

R28 therefore separates:

```text
CulturalItem =
a socially transmitted / socially stabilized
belief, value, skill, practice, sign-relation, norm,
artifact form, role script or other behavioral/cognitive variant.
```

from:

```text
CulturalSystem(P, D, t) =
a population/network-level distribution of CulturalItems
+ transmission/reproduction pathways
+ public/external carriers
+ recurrent interpretive/expectational structure
+ enough persistence through time
that cultural differences alter future cognition/action.
```

`P` is a population/network, `D` a domain, and `t` time.

A Culture can be narrow:

```text
one guild's greeting convention
```

or broad:

```text
interlocking language / ritual / status / craft / memory / norm system.
```

Do not require maximal breadth before using the term.

---

# 3. Culture — distributed socially reproduced structure

R28's working definition:

```text
Culture(P, D, t) =
relatively persistent socially transmitted/reproduced structure
in how a population/network perceives, interprets, values,
coordinates, creates or acts,
carried across distributed Subject states
and/or public practices, artifacts, symbols and institutions.
```

Important consequences:

```text
Culture need not exist in one database row.
Culture need not be known by every member.
Culture need not be consensual.
Culture need not be consciously articulated.
Culture need not be homogeneous.
Culture need not be ancient.
Culture need not be adaptive.
```

---

# 4. Culture is population/network state, not a Subject field

A Subject can have:

```text
CulturalKnowledge
CulturalCompetence
CulturalIdentification
CulturalPractice
```

but no single Subject contains the whole Culture.

A useful decomposition:

```text
CulturalState =
DistributedInternalizations
+ PublicPractices
+ ExternalArtifacts / Places / Media
+ Symbolic / Semantic Conventions
+ NormativeExpectations
+ TransmissionTopology
+ CollectiveMemory / Provenance
```

Institutions can carry part of this state but are not identical to it.

---

# 5. Culture != Group

A group is a set/collective relation among Subjects.

Culture is a transmitted pattern across Subjects/public structures.

Therefore:

```text
one Group can contain multiple Cultures/Subcultures
one Culture can span multiple Groups
one Subject can participate in multiple Cultures
```

This rejects the common game shortcut:

```text
FactionId == CultureId
```

---

# 6. Culture != Consensus / Homogeneity

Culture can contain:

```text
majority practice
minority interpretation
contested symbol
counter-norm
subculture
regional variant
generational variation
```

A society can share the fact that a symbol matters while disagreeing about what it should mean.

Thus:

```text
SharedCulturalSignificance
can coexist with
InterpretiveConflict.
```

---

# 7. Cultural Distribution

R28 introduces:

```text
CulturalDistribution(item, population, t) =
how a cultural item/practice/interpretation
is distributed across Subjects / groups / locations / roles.
```

Useful dimensions:

```text
frequency
intensity / commitment
competence
group concentration
network centrality
age/generation
role/status
location
visibility
```

Do not compress culture to one percentage unless that percentage is the intended mechanic.

---

# 8. Cultural Topology

R28 introduces:

```text
CulturalTopology =
Subjects / groups / institutions / artifacts as nodes
+
transmission / participation / interpretation / sanction edges.
```

Important questions:

```text
Who learns from whom?
Who performs with whom?
Who can interpret which symbols?
Who sanctions whom?
Which artifacts preserve practice?
Which boundaries block or reshape transmission?
```

Culture can therefore be network-local rather than global.

---

# 9. Convention — history-supported coordination regularity

R19 already observed:

```text
repeated interaction
→ convention
→ reduced coordination cost
```

R28 sharpens:

```text
Convention =
a recurrent coordination solution among viable alternatives
sustained substantially because participants expect
relevant others to use / recognize the same solution.
```

Examples:

```text
drive left versus right
which emote means “ready”
where traders meet
how raid roles are announced
which token counts as a bid
```

---

# 10. Convention != Rule / Norm / Equilibrium

```text
Rule
= authoritative constraint/transition prescription.

Equilibrium
= stability property under a specified deviation model.

Convention
= historically/socially selected recurrent solution
whose use is expectation-dependent.

Norm
= shared prescriptive expectation + distributed social enforcement/evaluation.
```

A convention can be an equilibrium, but `Equilibrium` is an analytical property rather than a cultural history.

A convention can later become a norm:

```text
“we coordinate this way”
→
“this is how one ought to do it here”
```

but the transition is not automatic.

---

# 11. Convention can be arbitrary without being meaningless

Several alternatives may be materially equivalent before coordination history:

```text
left / right
red / blue token
hand sign A / B
```

Once expectations stabilize, switching unilaterally becomes costly.

Therefore:

```text
ArbitraryOrigin != NoConsequence
```

and:

```text
ConventionalMeaning can become deeply consequential
even when physical form is arbitrary.
```

---

# 12. Custom — socially recognized recurrent practice

R28 uses:

```text
Custom =
a recurrent socially recognized practice
typical/expected in a population or context.
```

A custom can be:

```text
weakly normative
conventional
expressive
instrumental
```

but none is required universally.

```text
Custom != individual Habit
```

because custom is socially distributed/recognized.

---

# 13. Repetition does not make Custom automatically

If ten unrelated Subjects independently choose the cheapest tool:

```text
same repeated behavior
```

may arise without social transmission or recognition.

Thus:

```text
BehavioralFrequency != Culture
```

Need evidence of social dependence:

```text
learning
expectation
imitation
recognition
coordination
transmission
public meaning
```

---

# 14. Tradition — continuity whose relation to the past matters

R28 working definition:

```text
Tradition =
a culturally transmitted practice / representation / form
whose continuity from prior bearers, generations or occasions
is itself relevant to its identity, meaning, authority or value.
```

This makes lineage/provenance central.

```text
PracticeRepeatedForYears
!= Tradition automatically
```

if nobody's current practice depends on inherited continuity.

---

# 15. Tradition != Oldness

A recently introduced practice can become a tradition when:

```text
prior performance
→ recognized lineage / expected recurrence
→ current meaning / authority
```

Conversely, an ancient behavior independently rediscovered without transmission may not be a living tradition.

Thus:

```text
Age != Traditionality
```

---

# 16. Tradition != unchanged form

Transmission is reconstructive/transformative.

A tradition can alter:

```text
performance
materials
language
participants
interpretation
```

while preserving enough lineage/recognition to count as continuation.

This is R24 identity logic applied to cultural forms:

```text
CulturalContinuity != QualitativeIdentity
```

---

# 17. Cultural Continuity

R28 introduces:

```text
CulturalContinuity(form_t1, form_t2) =
enough transmission / lineage / recognition / functional continuity
for a relevant community to treat later practice/form
as continuation of the earlier one.
```

Possible channels:

```text
performer lineage
teacher lineage
text/artifact continuity
institutional recognition
symbolic continuity
functional role
shared narrative
place/calendar continuity
```

No one channel is universal.

---

# 18. Ritual — culturally patterned meaningful performance

R28 working definition:

```text
Ritual =
a culturally patterned performance
whose form/occasion/participation is socially recognized
and whose meaning or social/normative/identity function
extends beyond its immediate instrumental mechanics.
```

Common but non-universal features:

```text
formalization
prescribed sequence
repetition
synchrony
causal opacity
symbolic action
role differentiation
special place/time
emotional arousal
witnessing
commitment
```

None alone defines Ritual.

---

# 19. Routine != Ritual

Same physical sequence:

```text
wash hands
stand in line
walk around table
repeat phrase
```

can be:

```text
Routine
```

if primarily repeated for instrumental efficiency,

or:

```text
Ritual
```

when culturally recognized form/performance carries symbolic, relational, identity, normative or institutional significance.

Therefore:

```text
ActionSequence alone does not determine Ritual status.
```

---

# 20. Ritual != Tradition

A ritual may be traditional, but:

```text
newly invented initiation ritual
```

can be a ritual before it has long lineage.

A tradition may be non-ritual:

```text
recipe
craft technique
storytelling style
annual competition rule
```

Thus:

```text
Ritual concerns meaningful patterned performance.
Tradition concerns transmitted continuity from the past.
```

---

# 21. Ritual can be performative

A ritual may not merely represent state; under recognized social/institutional rules it can **create** state.

Examples:

```text
oath
initiation
coronation
marriage rite
funeral
peace ceremony
```

Generic abstraction:

```text
RecognizedPerformance
+ valid participants/context
→ Social / Relational / Identity / Institutional StateChange
```

This connects R20 Authorship/Expression, R24 Identity, R25 Relationship and R15 Institution.

---

# 22. Ritual Causality

R28 introduces:

```text
RitualCausality =
otherwise similar action sequence
+ different cultural recognition/context
→ different social / identity / relational / institutional consequence.
```

A button animation with no recognized consequences is presentation.

A kneeling sequence that changes rank, oath, trust or legitimacy can be gameplay.

---

# 23. Synchrony is a mechanism, not the definition of ritual

Experimental ritual work shows that synchronized action, especially with shared intentionality/arousal in some contexts, can alter cooperation/cohesion.

But:

```text
Synchrony != Ritual
Arousal != Ritual
CooperationEffect != RitualDefinition
```

Use mechanisms only when the intended cultural/relational consequence needs them.

---

# 24. Sign, signal and symbol

R17 already owns information signals.

R28 uses:

```text
Signal =
an observable event/state made available to an observer
that can change observation/belief/policy.
```

R28 adds:

```text
Sign =
a perceivable form interpreted as standing for,
indicating or invoking something beyond itself
within an interpretive context.
```

and:

```text
Symbol =
a Sign whose reliable interpretation depends substantially
on learned/conventional cultural relations rather than
only direct physical causation or resemblance.
```

---

# 25. Symbol != Meaning

A symbol is a carrier/form in an interpretive relation.

Meaning is the interpretation/significance relation.

```text
SymbolForm
+ Interpreter / CulturalContext
→ Meaning / ExpectedUse
```

Therefore:

```text
same SymbolForm
→ different meanings across groups/times
```

and:

```text
different forms
→ same meaning
```

are both possible.

---

# 26. Symbol != Identifier

R24 Identifier binds referent identity under some authority.

A Symbol may instead carry:

```text
value
memory
status
solidarity
threat
sacredness
role
ideology
```

A flag can identify a faction **and** symbolize sacrifice/home/authority; these are different relations.

---

# 27. Meaning is relational, not intrinsic to form

R28 uses:

```text
Meaning(S, X, C) =
interpretive / evaluative / practical significance
that X has for Subject S in context C.
```

Possible layers:

```text
referential
instrumental
normative
identity
relational
emotional
aesthetic
narrative
strategic
```

R8 and R20 remain active: meaning cannot be reduced to referential content.

---

# 28. Shared Meaning

R28 defines:

```text
SharedMeaning(P, X, C) =
sufficiently overlapping and mutually expected
interpretation/use/significance relations
among relevant participants
that X can support recurrent coordination,
communication, identity or social consequence.
```

Crucially:

```text
SharedMeaning != IdenticalPrivateRepresentation
```

Subjects can understand enough to coordinate while imagining or valuing X differently.

---

# 29. Shared Belief != Shared Meaning

Everyone can believe:

```text
“The tower is 20 meters tall.”
```

without the tower having shared cultural significance.

Conversely, a flag can possess shared cultural significance while Subjects disagree about factual claims surrounding it.

Thus:

```text
PropositionalAgreement != CulturalMeaning
```

---

# 30. Shared Meaning != Consensus

A contested symbol can be culturally powerful precisely because competing groups attach incompatible evaluations to the same referent.

Useful structure:

```text
SharedReferentialRecognition
+
DivergentEvaluation / NormativeMeaning
```

Culture can include the conflict itself.

---

# 31. Common ground and convention

Communication does not require infinite common knowledge.

A practical game-facing condition is:

```text
Enough mutual expectation about interpretation/action
for current coordination to succeed.
```

Misyak-style instantaneous conventions pressure-test the idea that interaction/context can stabilize a temporary meaning even when the signal form itself is minimal.

Thus:

```text
SharedMeaning can be local / temporary / dyadic
without becoming population-wide Culture immediately.
```

Persistence/transmission determines broader cultural status.

---

# 32. Cultural semantics can be learned through use

A Subject may not receive a dictionary.

```text
observe use
→ infer meaning
→ participate
→ receive social feedback
→ revise interpretation
```

This is R27 Learning applied to cultural semantics.

Playable culture can therefore use inference rather than exposition.

---

# 33. Legitimacy — recognized rightfulness/appropriateness

R28 separates sociological legitimacy from objective moral philosophy.

Working game definition:

```text
Legitimacy(A, Public, Domain, t) =
distributed recognition/belief that A
is entitled, proper or appropriate to exercise
a specified authority / rule / practice
within that reference public and domain.
```

This is observer/public-relative social state.

---

# 34. Legality != Legitimacy

```text
Legality = conformity to recognized formal law/rule authority.

Legitimacy = social/normative recognition of rightfulness/appropriateness.
```

Possible cases:

```text
legal + legitimate
legal + widely viewed illegitimate
illegal/usurping + socially accepted legitimate
powerful + illegitimate
legitimate claim + weak enforcement power
```

Games often collapse all of these into a faction ownership flag; R28 rejects that.

---

# 35. Power != Authority != Legitimacy

```text
Power = causal ability to alter futures.

Authority = recognized/authorized decision-right or jurisdiction
under a social/institutional structure.

Legitimacy = recognition that such authority/rule/practice
is rightful/appropriate within a reference order.
```

A warlord may have Power without Authority.

A formally appointed official may have Authority but little Legitimacy among one population.

---

# 36. Compliance != Legitimacy

Subjects may comply because of:

```text
fear
reward
lack of alternatives
habit
coordination
legitimacy
```

Therefore:

```text
ObservedCompliance does not reveal its motivational source.
```

Likewise legitimate authority can face noncompliance due competing values, incapacity or strategic incentives.

---

# 37. Legitimacy sources are plural

Do not freeze one universal source.

Possible legitimacy support:

```text
fair procedure
tradition
recognized succession
shared identity
norm/value alignment
performance/outcomes
expertise
consent
participation
sacred authority
predictability / taken-for-grantedness
```

Different publics can weight them differently.

---

# 38. Legitimacy Topology

R28 introduces:

```text
LegitimacyTopology =
which publics / groups / institutions
recognize which authority/rule/practice
as legitimate in which domains.
```

Thus:

```text
Legitimacy != one global scalar.
```

A rebel leader can be legitimate to one region and illegitimate to another.

---

# 39. Legitimacy Causality

R28 introduces:

```text
LegitimacyCausality =
same formal rule / enforcement capability
+ different distributed legitimacy state
→ different voluntary compliance, cooperation,
information trust, resistance, sanction or succession behavior.
```

This makes legitimacy gameplay-relevant.

---

# 40. Legitimacy != moral rightness

R28 uses legitimacy primarily in the sociological/experienced sense.

```text
A population can regard an unjust system as legitimate.
A morally defensible reform can initially lack legitimacy.
```

Therefore:

```text
SociologicalLegitimacy != MoralCorrectness
```

A Game may separately model moral/normative judgment if needed.

---

# 41. Collective Memory — culture about the past

R13:

```text
History = past events whose causal traces remain in the present.
Memory = past-derived information available to cognition/action.
```

R28 adds:

```text
CollectiveMemory(P, Past) =
socially maintained selection, framing, representation,
commemoration and transmission of past events/forms
that remains group-relevant in present interpretation/action.
```

---

# 42. Collective Memory != History

World History may contain:

```text
E1 E2 E3 E4 E5
```

while a group collectively remembers:

```text
E1 + E4
```

through a particular framing.

Another group may emphasize:

```text
E2 + E5
```

Thus:

```text
HistoricalTruth
!= CollectiveMemoryContent
```

without requiring either memory to be deliberately false.

---

# 43. Collective Memory != aggregate identical memories

No group mind is required.

Collective memory can be carried through:

```text
stories
archives
monuments
holidays
rituals
songs
place names
museums
family transmission
institutional records
artifacts
```

Subjects can know different subsets yet participate in a common memory structure.

---

# 44. Archive != Collective Memory

An archive is stored external information.

It becomes collective memory when:

```text
selection / retrieval / interpretation / transmission
→ present group-relevant consequence.
```

Thus R27 applies again:

```text
StoredPast != FunctionalMemory
```

at a social scale.

---

# 45. Collective Memory can outlive witnesses

```text
DirectWitnesses = 0
```

while:

```text
monument
ritual
story
schooling
institution
```

continue to reproduce a past framing.

This is one way culture crosses generations without requiring biological continuity of specific Subjects.

---

# 46. Collective Memory can be contested

Possible structure:

```text
same historical event
→ official memory
→ family memory
→ minority counter-memory
→ foreign memory
```

These can coexist and compete over:

```text
identity
victimhood
credit
blame
territory
legitimacy
obligation
```

Collective memory therefore belongs naturally to R19 strategy and R24 identity as well as R13 history.

---

# 47. Commemoration

R28 uses:

```text
Commemoration =
public/cultural action that reactivates, frames and transmits
selected past significance in the present.
```

A commemoration can be ritual, but need not be.

It can update collective memory by changing:

```text
salience
interpretation
participants
symbol use
legitimacy
identity
```

# 48. Cultural Transmission

R27 defined Learning for one Subject.

R28 defines:

```text
CulturalTransmission =
a causal path by which one Subject/group/public carrier's
cultural state or behavior contributes to another Subject's
acquisition/reconstruction of a cultural item/practice/meaning.
```

Mechanisms:

```text
imitation
teaching
language
observation
participation
ritual
artifact use
institutional instruction
sanction/reward
media
```

---

# 49. Transmission Topology

R28 introduces:

```text
CulturalTransmissionTopology =
who can acquire which cultural variants
from whom / what carrier,
through which channel,
with what fidelity/transformation/bias.
```

Classic transmission directions can include:

```text
vertical       parent/older lineage → descendant
horizontal     peer → peer
oblique        older/nonparent/institution → learner
```

Game worlds can use arbitrary network equivalents.

---

# 50. Social learning != Cultural Transmission by itself

One Subject imitating another once is social learning.

A cultural pattern requires enough:

```text
persistence
reproduction
population distribution
or public consequence
```

for the learned variant to become part of a broader cultural structure.

Thus:

```text
SocialLearningEvent != CulturalSystem
```

# 51. Transmission != exact copy

Bartlett and iterated-learning work make this especially important.

```text
SourceForm
→ observation / reconstruction
→ LearnerForm
```

may change:

```text
surface form
complexity
meaning
salience
structure
```

So:

```text
CulturalTransmission
can include transformation.
```

# 52. Cultural fidelity is multidimensional

A practice can preserve:

```text
function
```

while changing form,

when relevant.

# 53. Cultural Innovation

```text

introduction of a culturally transmissible variant/form/interpretation
```

Sources can include:


# 54. Adoption

```text

into S's usable repertoire / practice / belief / interpretation.
```

Adoption is not necessarily endorsement.

```text

```

# 55. Internalization

R28 uses cautiously:

```text
Internalization =
a cultural rule/value/meaning becomes part of

not merely an externally recognized constraint.
```

Therefore:

```text

ComplyNorm != InternalizeNorm
```

This connects R18 Value and R27 Persistent Subject Change.

# 56. Diffusion

```text
CulturalDiffusion =
spread of cultural variants/practices across a population/network.
```

Diffusion depends on:

```text

exposure
status/prestige
payoff perception
conformity
content salience
identity boundary
institutional support
translation/compatibility
```

No universal adoption function.

# 57. Transmission biases

Useful categories, not universal primitives:

```text

```

Game rule:

# 58. Cultural Drift

```text
CulturalDrift =
change in variant distribution caused substantially by
sampling/finite-population/transmission stochasticity
rather than a stable directional adoption advantage.
```

Do not force every cultural trend to have a hidden rational purpose.

---

# 59. Cultural Selection

```text
CulturalSelection =
systematic differential transmission/adoption/retention
of variants because of their content, consequences,
carriers, institutions or social environment.
```

This is an analytical abstraction.

R28 does **not** assume every detail of biological natural selection transfers to culture.

---

# 60. Transformation

```text
CulturalTransformation =
variant meaning/form/function changes during
learning, reconstruction, use or recombination.
```

Selection and transformation can operate together.

This protects Game from naive “meme = immutable gene” metaphors.

# 62. Cumulative Culture

R28 uses:

```text
CumulativeCulture =
a transmission process in which retained prior cultural modifications
become inputs/constraints/resources for later modifications,
allowing later forms/capabilities to depend on inherited cultural history.
```

Strong test:

```text
Could a new isolated Subject easily reconstruct the current form
without inheriting prior cultural state?
```

If not, cumulative dependence may be high.

# 63. Cultural accumulation != cumulative culture

```text
100 unrelated songs added to archive
```

is accumulation.

```text

→ compositional techniques
→ inherited theory
→ later works impossible/very costly without prior layers
```

is closer to cumulative culture.

Thus:

```text

```

# 88. Same behavior != same culture

Two isolated populations can independently converge on:

```text
wear warm clothing
trade at noon
use a red warning mark
```

Same behavior/function does not prove shared transmission/history/meaning.

Thus:

```text
BehavioralSimilarity
!= SharedCulture
```

This is a major falsifier for simulation systems that infer “culture” from clustering alone.

---

# 89. Same culture can support different behavior

Members can share:

```text
language
ritual calendar
historical memory
symbolic repertoire
```

while disagreeing politically or behaving differently by role/status/context.

Therefore:

```text
Culture does not uniquely determine Policy.
```

It supplies learned structure/priors/affordances/constraints, not deterministic scripts.

# 90. Cultural essentialism failure

Failure:

```text
Faction = “warrior culture”
→ every member aggressive
```

Result:

```text
culture becomes personality stereotype / species essence.
```

R28 requires population distributions, role/context variation and learning history where depth matters.

# 91. Cultural Agency

R28 introduces:

```text
CulturalAgency =
capacity to adopt, reject, interpret, perform, preserve,
teach, transmit, contest, remix or transform cultural forms
with meaningful future consequence.
```

This is not freedom from culture.

A player can exercise cultural agency by choosing how to participate in inherited structure.

---

# 92. Cultural Causality

R28's central test:

```text
CulturalCausality =
otherwise comparable physical/current state
+ different relevant cultural structure/history
→ materially different interpretation,
affordance, expectation, legitimacy,
relationship, policy or consequence.
```

If culture can be removed without changing any future, it is likely lore/presentation rather than gameplay state.

---

# 93. Symbolic Causality

Special case:

```text
same physical object/form
+ different conventional symbolic meaning
→ different Subject/social response.
```

Examples:

```text
cloth → ordinary material / sacred flag
ring  → decoration / marriage marker
color → pigment / mourning status
```

This is the game-facing proof that symbol meaning is causal.

---

# 94. Tradition Causality

```text
same current practice form
+ different recognized lineage/provenance
→ different legitimacy / identity / value / obligation.
```

A newly copied crown and an inherited coronation object may be materially identical but culturally distinct.

---

# 95. Collective-memory causality

```text
same present material conditions
+ different socially transmitted framing of past
→ different identity / trust / legitimacy / territorial claim / policy.
```

This makes collective memory a real world layer rather than lore archive.

---

# 96. Playable Culture

R28 introduces:

```text
PlayableCulture =
cultural structure whose relevant conventions,
practices, symbols, meanings, memories,
legitimacy and transmission/change processes
can be observed/inferred/participated in/influenced enough
that cultural differences change meaningful future possibilities.
```

The player need not understand every cultural detail.

# 98. Cultural legibility

Players need enough evidence to infer:


```text
100 pages faction history
```

with no consequences can create world texture but not deep cultural gameplay.

```text

```

# 100. Minimum cultural complexity

Not a maturity ladder.

## CU0 — no persistent cultural structure

Behavior is individual/fixed/systemic without socially relevant transmission/meaning.

## CU1 — local convention / sign

```text
one learned recurrent coordination/sign relation
```

## CU2 — practice + norm/symbol/transmission

A population has multiple culturally learned practices/meanings with observable consequences.

## CU3 — tradition / collective memory / subculture

Cultural lineage, historical framing and network-local differentiation matter.

## CU4 — legitimacy / contestation / cultural change

Actors can contest symbols, norms, memories and authority recognition; culture changes endogenously.

## CU5 — multi-cultural ecology

```text
overlapping cultures
transmission topology
innovation/diffusion/loss/hybridization
multi-generation cumulative structure
```

Rule:

```text
Increase cultural complexity
only when it creates a new playable cultural counterfactual.
```

# 169. Direct answers

### What is Culture relative to socially learned information?

A cultural item can be socially learned information/practice. A cultural system is the persistent distributed population/network structure through which such items are transmitted, publicly carried, interpreted, reproduced and transformed with future consequences.

### Does everybody have to share the same belief?

No. Culture is distributed and can contain minority variants, subcultures and contested meanings.

### Convention versus Norm?

Convention is an expectation-dependent recurrent coordination solution among alternatives. Norm adds prescriptive expectation and distributed social evaluation/enforcement. Either can exist without the other in some cases.

### Custom versus Tradition?

Custom is a socially recognized recurrent practice. Tradition additionally makes transmitted continuity from prior bearers/time relevant to identity, meaning, authority or value.

### Ritual versus Routine?

Routine repeats chiefly for instrumental organization/efficiency. Ritual is culturally patterned performance whose recognized meaning/social/normative/identity function extends beyond immediate mechanics.

### Ritual versus Tradition?

Ritual concerns meaningful performance; Tradition concerns inherited continuity. They often overlap but neither implies the other.

### What makes a Symbol?

A sign becomes symbolic when interpretation depends substantially on learned/conventional cultural relations rather than physical form alone.

### Shared Meaning versus Shared Belief?

Shared belief is propositional overlap. Shared meaning is enough overlapping and mutually expected interpretation/use/significance for recurring communication/coordination/identity/social consequence.

### Does Shared Meaning require identical mental content?

No. Functional/inter-subjective overlap can coexist with private variation and disagreement.

### Legitimacy versus Legality?

Legality concerns formal rule conformity; legitimacy concerns distributed recognition of rightfulness/appropriateness in a reference public/domain.

### Legitimacy versus Power?

Power is causal capability; legitimacy is recognition that exercise of authority is rightful. Either can be high while the other is low.

### What is Collective Memory?

Socially maintained selection/framing/representation/transmission of the past that changes present group interpretation/action. It is not History itself, an archive, or identical memory in every member.

### Can culture change while remaining the same culture/tradition?

Yes. Cultural continuity can persist through lineage/recognition/function/meaning despite transformations in surface form.

### Does cultural transmission require exact copying?

No. Reconstruction, compression and transformation are normal possibilities; persistence can preserve function/meaning/lineage with surface change.

### What is cumulative culture?

Later cultural capabilities/forms build on retained prior modifications such that current structure depends on inherited cultural history, rather than merely accumulating more content.

### Can culture disappear?

Yes. Transmission failure can produce loss even if historical records remain. Archive survival is not living competence/tradition survival.

### Does an authored game world have Culture if it does not simulate cultural evolution online?

Yes. Diegetic culture can be authored and causally real. Online cultural evolution is optional machinery.

### Do identical Agent prompts constitute Culture?

They can encode an authored cultural prior/presentation, but do not demonstrate social cultural transmission. Common implementation is not the same as socially inherited history.

### When is Culture gameplay?

When cultural differences in convention, symbol, norm, memory, legitimacy, ritual, practice or transmission change meaningful future possibilities and can be learned/participated in/influenced enough to matter.
