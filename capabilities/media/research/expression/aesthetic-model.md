# Cross-medium aesthetic model

This document is the Aesthetic Laboratory inside the broader Art & Expression Laboratory. It asks how a work becomes perceptually coherent, interesting, expressive, memorable, or beautiful without pretending those outcomes are the same thing.

It is a production synthesis, not a universal theory of beauty.

## Keep the causal layers separate

```text
artifact properties
        ↓
perceptual processing
        ↓
affect / valuation
        ↓
meaning / interpretation
        ↓
aesthetic judgment
        ↓
behavior / memory / action
```

These layers interact, but collapsing them produces bad design rules. Edge density is an artifact diagnostic, not beauty. Processing fluency may increase pleasure without maximizing interest. A work can be aversive yet artistically powerful. A memorable artifact need not be the most immediately liked.

## Nine working dimensions

### 1. Perceptual legibility

Can the intended sensory structure be recovered without avoidable friction? Processing-fluency research supports legibility, contrast, grouping, repetition, symmetry, and learned prototypes as useful contributors to positive response in many contexts.

**Prior:** make the intended structure recoverable before spending complexity on expression.

**Boundary:** maximum fluency is not maximum beauty. Extreme ease can erase challenge, ambiguity, surprise, or character.

### 2. Coherence

Do the parts establish an intelligible whole? Coherence includes hierarchy, grouping, rhythm, repetition, proportion, continuity, and other relationships that let variation remain interpretable.

### 3. Richness

Is there enough meaningful differentiation and information to sustain attention? Variety, texture, density, color, contrast, and structural differentiation can all add richness.

The productive question is usually not `simple or complex?`, but:

> How much richness can this audience, medium, and moment integrate without losing the intended model?

### 4. Novelty

How does the work depart from learned category expectations? Hekkert, Snelders, and van Wieringen's typicality/novelty experiments support a useful tension: recognizability can provide entry while novelty creates discovery and identity.

### 5. Affect and valuation

What emotional, hedonic, aversive, arousal, reward, or approach response does the work invite? Pleasantness is only one possible target. Horror, tragedy, awe, suspense, unease, intimacy, and solemnity deliberately use other regions.

### 6. Meaning and context

What associations, cultural conventions, expertise, prior exposure, narrative, authorship, intent, and surrounding alternatives change the experience? Leder-style information-processing models and the aesthetic-triad literature both make meaning/knowledge part of aesthetic experience rather than decoration added after perception.

### 7. Craft and resolution

Does execution feel controlled and sufficiently resolved for the intended form? Craft includes precision, medium handling, typography, color/light control, transitions, timing, sound balance, finish, and deliberate roughness where roughness is the intended form.

### 8. Expression and presence

Does the work carry distinctive character or felt force beyond correctness? This is where a technically excellent but generic artifact often fails. Expression can arise from composition, material, rhythm, voice, timing, contrast, meaning, imperfection, or deliberate violation of convention.

### 9. Aftereffect

What remains after first contact: memory, curiosity, reflection, trust, desire, emotional residue, or action? First-impression preference is useful but too narrow for Studio as a general creative system.

## Aesthetic value often lives in tensions

The laboratory treats these as generative axes rather than independent variables to maximize:

| Tension | One failure mode | Opposite failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| fluency ↔ challenge | sterile / obvious | inaccessible / exhausting |
| unity ↔ variety | repetitive / dead | fragmented / chaotic |
| familiarity ↔ novelty | generic | alien / hard to enter |
| predictability ↔ surprise | boring | arbitrary |
| continuity ↔ discontinuity | flat | incoherent |
| restraint ↔ expression | forgettable | overwhelming |
| explicitness ↔ ambiguity | literal | opaque |
| density ↔ breathing room | cluttered | empty / under-articulated |

The correct answer is not always the midpoint. Intent may legitimately push toward an extreme.

## Why context is part of the model

Color preference research shows that learned object associations influence preference; artwork studies show contextual information can alter aesthetic engagement; Web studies show that prototypicality and complexity matter differently under fast first-impression conditions. Aesthetic response therefore cannot be modeled from the artifact alone.

At minimum retain:

```text
audience / observer class
medium
encounter mode / task
culture and learned convention
expertise
prior exposure
intent / genre
physical or interface environment
time / style context
```

## Agent decision model

```text
intent + medium + audience + context
                ↓
retrieve evidence and precedents
                ↓
choose desired outcomes + tension profile
                ↓
generate / compose
                ↓
mechanical and semantic checks
                ↓
Agent critique across aesthetic dimensions
                ↓
uncertainty test
        ┌───────┴─────────┐
        │                 │
    sufficient       unresolved claim
     evidence         about human response
        │                 │
        │           targeted calibration
        │                 │
        └────────┬────────┘
                 ↓
          publication judgment
                 ↓
          observed consequence
                 ↓
             revise prior
```

Human response is therefore evidence about human response, not a permanent creative-control boundary.
