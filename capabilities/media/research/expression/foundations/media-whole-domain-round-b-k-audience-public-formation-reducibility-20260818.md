# 2. The candidate already splits into two different ontological questions

## 2.1 Audience question

`Audience` usually names a **role relation to a mediated object/event/performance/system**:

```text
Bearer X is audience-relative-to M/E under role/scope Σ
```

This is often episode-, object- or channel-relative.

## 2.2 Public-formation question

`Public` usually names a **social formation** that can persist across many Media episodes and may be constituted through shared issues, discourse, circulation, consequences, identity, institutions or political relations.

Therefore:

```text
AudienceRoleStanding
≠ PublicFormationStanding
```

The combined residual must not be admitted as one Foundation unless a common irreducible relation survives both.

---

# 3. Audience does not require publicness

Counterexamples:

```text
private theatre screening
one-to-one voice message
closed classroom presentation
subscriber-only stream
confidential board briefing
private agent-to-agent report
```

All can have audiences without public access/status.

Thus:

```text
AudienceStanding
↛ PublicAccessStanding
```

B-D remains intact.

---

# 4. Public access does not require an audience occurrence

B-D already established:

```text
public zero-visit page
unread public notice
unused public endpoint
```

Therefore:

```text
PublicAccessStanding
↛ ActualAudienceOccurrence
```

Public access is a status/eligibility relation; audience occurrence is an episode/reception relation.

---

# 5. Intended audience ≠ actual audience

A speech can target citizens but be heard by journalists, tourists and opponents.

A private message can be leaked to outsiders.

A technical manual can be written for engineers but later consumed by agents.

Thus:

```text
IntendedAudience
≠ ActualAudience
```

Intended/target audience is an MF3 represented target-role or MF8 production intention; actual audience depends on B-B encounter/recruitment plus role standing.

---

# 6. Addressee ≠ audience member universally

A named addressee may never receive or perceive the message.

Conversely an overhearer/eavesdropper may perceive and interpret it despite not being addressed.

B-A already distinguished addressee, participant, overhearer and eavesdropper statuses.

Therefore:

```text
AddressedRecipient
≠ ActualAudienceMember
```

---

# 7. Delivery ≠ audience

A delivered email may never be opened.

A rendered frame may be technically delivered while the human is absent.

A message may arrive in an agent inbox but never be recruited into active context.

Therefore:

```text
DeliveredTo(X,M)
↛ AudienceEpisode(X,M)
```

Network/B-B stage separation survives.

---

# 8. Exposure ≠ audience role universally

A person can be exposed incidentally to a display without being socially/pragmatically treated as part of the intended audience.

Conversely an institutional audience role can be assigned even when an individual misses a particular exposure episode.

Therefore distinguish:

```text
ExposureOccurrence
AudienceRoleStanding
```

Audience is not merely a photometric/sensory event.

---

# 9. Audience role can exist with one member

A private recital for one listener, one reader of a report, or one agent consuming a generated analysis can form a valid audience relation.

Thus:

```text
AudienceCardinality > 1
```

is not necessary.

Audience is not a mass noun by ontology.

---

# 10. One bearer can occupy several audience roles simultaneously

A journalist can be:

```text
viewer of a livestream
professional observer of a politician
member of a specialist policy public
publisher to another audience
```

in the same period.

Therefore audience roles overlap and are scope-relative.

MF4 already allows overlapping compositions/communities and rejects unique partition assumptions.

---

# 11. Audience identity is episode/object relative

The same set of people watching Film A and attending Town Hall B may form:

```text
Audience(Film A)
Audience(Town Hall B)
```

with different roles despite identical member sets.

Therefore:

```text
AudienceIdentity
≠ MemberSetIdentity
```

The target object/event and role relation matter.

---

# 12. Same audience role can survive member turnover

A newspaper's readership, theatre audience category, subscriber audience or broadcast audience can persist as a social/operational audience type while individual members change.

Thus:

```text
AudienceTypeContinuity
≠ ExactMemberContinuity
```

MF7 typed continuation handles this.

---

# 13. Measured audience ≠ actual audience

Analytics may infer audience through:

```text
page views
cookies
accounts
ratings panels
sensor counts
agent logs
survey samples
```

Each measurement can omit, duplicate or misclassify consumers.

Therefore:

```text
AudienceMeasurement
≠ AudienceOccurrence
```

B-F measurement/selection and MF1 observation remain epistemic evidence layers.

---

# 14. Inferred audience ≠ intended audience

A platform may infer `sports fans` from behavior even though neither creators nor users explicitly adopted that identity.

Thus:

```text
TargetAudienceRepresentation
InferredAudienceModel
ActualAudienceEpisode
SelfIdentifiedCommunity
```

must remain distinct.

---

# 15. Market segment ≠ audience

A marketing segment can be defined before any Media encounter:

```text
18–24 year-old urban consumers
high-propensity subscribers
enterprise buyers
```

It is a classification/targeting construct.

Only when related to a Media object/event does it become a target-audience candidate.

Thus:

```text
MarketSegment
≠ AudienceStanding
```

Finance/market ownership remains external.

---

# 16. Follower/subscriber ≠ current audience

A follower may never see a post because of ranking, inactivity or filtering.

A non-follower may encounter it via search/repost/link.

Therefore:

```text
SubscriptionRelation
≠ AudienceOccurrence
```

B-F selection and B-B encounter remain separate.

---

# 17. Audience does not require interpretation agreement

Two viewers can watch the same film and derive opposite interpretations.

Therefore:

```text
AudienceMembership
↛ SharedInterpretation
```

B-G already separates representation content from consumer interpretation.

---

# 18. Audience does not require active participation

Spectatorship/listening/reading can be socially recognized audience roles without contribution back to the producer/public discourse.

Thus:

```text
Audience
↛ Participation
```

Audience cannot be defined only through interaction.

---

# 19. Participation does not imply audience

A technician operating a broadcast system, an editor modifying a feed, or a bot posting into a channel participates in production/distribution but need not be an audience member for the content.

Thus:

```text
Participation
↛ Audience
```

Role typing is mandatory.

---

# 20. Audience may be human, artificial, or mixed at the functional Media level

A report can be consumed by:

```text
human analyst
search indexer
LLM agent
monitoring daemon
robotic controller
human-agent team
```

where the downstream system recruits distinctions as a consumer.

Therefore a functional Media ontology should permit:

```text
Consumer/AudienceBearerType
= human | artificial | collective | hybrid
```

without claiming phenomenal/social equivalence among bearer types.

MF0 recruitment + MF3 content + consumer role suffice.

---

# 21. Functional machine audience ≠ human social public

A set of agents receiving the same telemetry may constitute a machine audience/consumer class without forming a `public` in the political/social sense.

Therefore:

```text
MachineAudience
↛ PublicFormation
```

unless additional social/institutional/public-standing relations are grounded.

This is an important anti-anthropomorphism boundary.

---

# 22. Public formation cannot be reduced to audience size

B-D already falsified `public = large audience`.

A huge private webinar can remain a bounded audience.

A small local issue group can form a real public around a matter of shared concern.

Thus:

```text
Cardinality
```

is neither sufficient nor necessary for public formation.

---

# 23. Public ≠ crowd

A physical crowd shares co-presence.

But a public may be geographically dispersed and temporally asynchronous.

Conversely a stadium crowd may share space without constituting one issue/public sphere.

Therefore:

```text
CoPresence
≠ PublicFormation
```

MF5 spatial grouping is insufficient.

---

# 24. Public ≠ mass

A mass audience can consist of isolated consumers with little or no mutual orientation.

A public may instead be structured by discourse, issue orientation, social recognition or institutional roles.

Therefore:

```text
MassReach
≠ PublicFormation
```

Broadcast scale is not public ontology.

---

# 25. Public ≠ group

A small organization/team can be a highly structured group but operate entirely privately.

A public can relate strangers who do not form a bounded membership group.

Michael Warner's public/counterpublic tradition explicitly pressures group-membership models: publics can be self-organized through address and circulation among strangers.

Thus:

```text
BoundedMembershipGroup
≠ Public universally
```

---

# 26. Public ≠ community

Community often implies stronger belonging, repeated social relations, shared practices or identity.

An issue public can include adversaries with little community solidarity.

Conversely a private community need not be public.

Therefore:

```text
CommunityStanding
≠ PublicStanding
```

though the two may overlap.

---

# 27. Public ≠ collective identity

A policy public can contain participants who identify with opposing camps and share no unified identity.

Thus:

```text
SharedCollectiveIdentity
```

is not universally necessary.

Identity can strengthen some group publics/counterpublics but cannot be foundational for every public.

---

# 28. Public ≠ consensus

Habermasian deliberative ideals emphasize rational-critical discussion, but actual publics can contain persistent conflict, disagreement and contestation.

Contemporary public-sphere research increasingly models networks of multiple, intersecting, adversarial publics rather than one harmonious sphere.

Therefore:

```text
Consensus
≠ PublicFormation
```

MF4 already rejects global harmony as constitutive of composition.

---

# 29. Public ≠ collective agency

MF8 explicitly establishes:

```text
Plurality ≠ CollectiveBearer
Coordination ≠ CollectiveAgency
JointAction ≠ PersistentCollectiveAgency
```

A public can exist without becoming a unified agent capable of choosing/acting as one bearer.

Thus:

```text
PublicFormation
↛ CollectiveAgencyStanding
```

A constituted committee may be both; an issue public usually need not be.

---

# 30. Collective agency ≠ public

A corporation or committee can satisfy strong collective-agency criteria while remaining private and not functioning as a public.

Therefore:

```text
CollectiveAgencyStanding
↛ PublicFormationStanding
```

The two axes are independent.

---

# 31. Public ≠ public opinion

A poll can aggregate individual responses into a distribution called `public opinion` without the respondents ever communicating with each other or forming a public around the issue.

Conversely a public can exist while opinion remains fragmented, unstable or unmeasured.

Thus:

```text
OpinionDistribution
≠ PublicFormation
```

and:

```text
PublicFormation
↛ ConsensusOpinion
```

---

# 32. Public opinion measurement ≠ public voice

A sample, summary, recommender metric or LLM synthesis can represent a population's responses but does not become the population/public itself.

Agent-era public-opinion analysis intensifies this boundary.

Therefore:

```text
RepresentationOfPublicOpinion
≠ Public
```

MF3 representation/evidence prevents reification.

---

# 33. Synthetic participants do not automatically constitute the represented human public

LLM agents can simulate personas, responses or deliberation participants.

Even if their aggregate distribution resembles observed human data:

```text
SyntheticPopulation
≠ HumanPublic
```

unless the research claim is explicitly a model/simulation standing.

This is a standing/evidence boundary, not merely an accuracy concern.

---

# 34. Habermas route — communicative public sphere

Habermas's public sphere is classically reconstructed as a social/institutional domain in which private individuals come together to communicate about matters of general concern and in which public opinion can form.

Important consequences:

```text
PublicSphere
≠ public-access resource set
≠ mass audience
≠ state institution
≠ market by identity
```

The public sphere mediates between domains and depends on communicative/social/institutional organization.

This is powerful evidence for a genuine high-level social formation, but not yet for a Media primitive.

---

# 35. Habermasian public-sphere standing is not necessary for every thing called `public`

Historical and contemporary scholarship recognizes:

```text
literary publics
issue publics
counterpublics
networked publics
specialist publics
affective publics
personal publics
```

with structures that do not all satisfy one deliberative-democratic ideal.

Therefore a normative Habermasian criterion cannot silently define all `PublicFormationStanding`.

---

# 36. Dewey route — consequences can generate a public

John Dewey's *The Public and Its Problems* takes a different route: a public forms from those sufficiently affected by indirect consequences of conjoint activity such that those consequences require systematic care/regulation.

This generates:

```text
AffectedByConsequences
+ Recognition/Concern
+ Organization/RegulationPressure
→ Public candidate
```

This is not identical to Warner's circulation route or Habermas's deliberative public sphere.

Therefore:

```text
Issue/Affectedness
```

can help constitute a public even before strong shared identity or mass-media audience standing exists.

---

# 37. Affectedness alone is not sufficient

Many people may be causally affected by pollution, software policy or market action without recognizing the issue or organizing/communicating around it.

Dewey himself discusses publics that remain inchoate/unorganized.

Therefore distinguish:

```text
AffectedPopulation
LatentPublicCandidate
RecognizedIssuePublic
OrganizedPublic
InstitutionalizedResponse
```

No one boolean `isPublic` captures the whole progression.

---

# 38. Media exposure is not necessary for affectedness

A person can be materially affected by an externality before receiving any Media representation of it.

Thus:

```text
AffectedByIssue
≠ AudienceOfIssueRepresentation
```

Media can help a latent public identify itself, but does not create the underlying affectedness.

This locates the issue relation partly in World/Human/Institution rather than Media.

---

# 39. Warner route — publics can arise through address and reflexive circulation

Warner's public/counterpublic tradition emphasizes publics that come into being in relation to texts/discourse and their circulation, including address to strangers and reflexive circulation over time.

This gives Media genuine constitutive importance in some public formations:

```text
Representations
+ Address
+ Circulation
+ Recurring Uptake/Response
+ Temporal Reflexivity
→ discourse-public formation route
```

But constitutive importance in a route does not imply a new primitive.

The constituents are already MF3/MF4/MF6/MF7/MF8 + Human/social standing.

---

# 40. Circulation is not sufficient for public formation

A spam campaign, automated log stream or repeated ad distribution can circulate texts extensively without constituting a meaningful public around them.

Therefore:

```text
Circulation
↛ PublicFormation
```

Additional social/relevance/address/recognition/practice relations are needed.

---

# 41. Circulation is not necessary under every public theory

A Deweyan affected public can begin from shared indirect consequences before robust discourse circulation.

A physically assembled local public can deliberate orally without durable text circulation.

Thus:

```text
ReflexiveTextCirculation
```

is a powerful public-formation route, not a universal primitive.

---

# 42. Public can form through oral/live mediation

Town meetings, assemblies, rituals, hearings and public speeches can support public formation without fixed artifacts.

Therefore:

```text
PublicFormation
↛ Fixation
```

B-H remains intact.

---

# 43. Public can persist across Media objects

A climate-policy public, scientific public or fandom can engage with thousands of changing messages/works/events.

Thus:

```text
PublicIdentity
≠ IdentityOfAnySingleMediaToken
```

MF7 temporal continuation + Human/social issue/identity standing is required.

---

# 44. One Media object can participate in several publics

The same article/video/report can circulate through:

```text
professional specialist public
political counterpublic
fandom
international press public
machine-analysis ecosystem
```

under different framing and issue relations.

Therefore:

```text
MediaObject
↛ unique Public
```

MF4 overlapping composition and B-G recontextualization handle this.

# 45. Same participant set can instantiate several publics

The same citizens can deliberate separately about:

```text
transport
school policy
AI regulation
local environment
```

with different issue frames, institutions and discursive histories.

Thus:

```text
PublicIdentity
≠ MemberSetIdentity
```

just as audience identity was not member-set identity.

---

# 46. Public membership can be fuzzy, latent and temporally variable

Participants can be:

```text
active contributors
regular readers
occasional responders
lurkers
former participants
new entrants
indirectly affected nonparticipants
```

Different theories classify these differently.

Therefore PublicFormation should support graded/role-typed participation evidence without treating one engagement threshold as universal ontology.

---

# 47. Public boundaries can overlap

Counterpublic, specialist public, national public, platform public and issue public can overlap in participants and discourse.

Contemporary communication research explicitly models a **network of publics**, not one exhaustive partition.

MF4's overlapping-composition result already admits this.

---

# 48. Public boundaries can be contested

Actors can disagree over:

```text
who counts as affected
who may speak
whose voice is representative
which issue frame is legitimate
whether a group is a public, lobby, mob, fandom or stakeholder set
```

Therefore:

```text
PublicBoundary
```

can itself be an object of social/institutional contestation.

A model must preserve claimed vs recognized vs observed boundary standing.

---

# 49. Counterpublics falsify one-unified-public ontology

Fraser/Warner traditions emphasize subordinate/oppositional/counterpublic formations.

These can develop distinct discourse, identity, spaces and circulation practices while interacting with wider publics.

Therefore:

```text
OneSociety → OnePublic
```

is false.

Plural and conflicting publics can be simultaneously real.

---

# 50. Counterpublic ≠ merely restricted/private group

A counterpublic can address broader society while maintaining alternative discourse and identity.

Its defining standing is not simply access restriction.

Thus B-D public/private status cannot reconstruct counterpublic formation alone.

Human/social/institutional relation is required.

---

# 51. Public formation can be issue-relative without stable collective identity

Recent communication research treats topic/issue publics as legitimate formations whose members orient around a shared issue rather than durable group identity.

Therefore:

```text
StableGroupIdentity
```

is not necessary.

The issue/relevance frame can provide continuity.

---

# 52. Group publics show the reverse route

Other publics can be organized around identity/belonging rather than a single issue.

Therefore:

```text
IssueOrientation
```

is not universally necessary either.

This is further evidence that `public` is a family of social-formation routes rather than one primitive relation.

---

# 53. Polity publics add institutional membership

Citizenship, jurisdiction and constitutional roles can constitute a polity-public relation.

Thus:

```text
PolityPublic
```

may depend strongly on Institution/Law rather than Media circulation.

Again no Media primitive survives across all public types.

---

# 54. Public formation does not require open access to every discourse token

Counterpublics, professional publics and restricted policy publics may have bounded spaces while still constituting publics under a broader social relation.

Therefore:

```text
PublicFormation
↛ EveryMediaObjectPubliclyAccessible
```

B-D access-publicness remains distinct.

---

# 55. Open access does not create a public

A publicly readable data dump can remain socially inert.

Therefore:

```text
PublicAccess
↛ PublicFormation
```

This is the direct converse.

---

# 56. Shared exposure is not sufficient

Millions can see the same weather alert or advertisement without forming a durable public.

Thus:

```text
SharedExposure
↛ PublicFormation
```

B-B exposure is necessary only for some formation routes/episodes.

---

# 57. Shared exact Media token is not necessary

Personalized feeds may give different participants different representations while they still orient to the same issue/event/institution.

Therefore:

```text
SamePublic
↛ SameBitstream / SameMediaToken
```

Public continuity may instead depend on shared referents/issues/relations across divergent representations.

This is increasingly important in Agent-era personalized media.

---

# 58. Shared referent is not universally sufficient

Opposing publics can orient to the same event while constructing radically different meanings, relevance frames and identities.

Recent work on conditional publics under geopolitical crisis explicitly observes that structural/public relations can depend on whether participants share an issue-specific referential frame or construct divergent meanings.

Therefore:

```text
SharedEventReference
↛ OnePublic
```

B-G meaning/context remains relevant.

---

# 59. Algorithmic co-selection can help assemble an audience without creating a public

Recommendation can repeatedly expose similar users to the same themes.

B-F already established selection as a derived policy/allocation profile.

Therefore:

```text
AlgorithmicallyAssembledAudience
↛ PublicFormation
```

unless additional social/discursive/issue/identity relations emerge.

# 60. Public formation can feed back into selection

Once a public becomes socially legible, platforms/journalists/agents can target or rank content for it.

This is one bridge into the still-open **Reflexive Mediation Ecology** residual.

# 61. Audience analytics can alter the audience/public being measured

without losing all continuity.

```text
PublicIdentity
```

# 63. Platforms can contain many publics and nonpublic audiences

One platform can host:

```text

mass spectators
issue publics

counterpublics
```

Thus:

```text
PlatformUserBase
```

# 64. Public formation can occur without modern mass media

Historical salons, assemblies, coffee houses, oral forums and local civic practices are enough to falsify a digital/mass-media necessity claim.

Habermas's historical reconstruction itself traces public-sphere formation through varied institutional/media settings rather than one technology.

Therefore:

```text
ModernMassMedia
```

is not constitutive.

---

# 65. Media can nevertheless be constitutive in particular public routes

Reduction must not become eliminativism.

For a Warner-style discourse public:

```text
address
representation
circulation
recirculation
response
```

are not incidental—they are part of how that public exists.

For networked publics, media affordances/infrastructures alter visibility, persistence, searchability, scalability and participant relations.

Therefore Media can be **constitutively involved** without owning the higher-level `PublicFormation` primitive.

---

# 66. Public formation is not reducible to communication alone

B-A reduced generic Communication.

A public additionally may require some combination of:

```text
issue/relevance relation
affectedness
participation/belonging
institutional role
collective identification
temporal circulation history
recognized social boundary
```

Therefore:

```text
CommunicationEpisode
↛ PublicFormation
```

Public is a higher-order social organization profile.

---

# 67. Public formation is not reducible to representation alone

Representations can specify `the public`, `citizens`, `fans`, `voters` or `followers` even when no corresponding public exists.

Thus:

```text
RepresentedPublicSet
≠ ActualPublicFormation
```

MF3 representation standing must not be reified into social ontology.

---

# 68. Public formation is not reducible to MF4 composition alone

An analyst can define a set/network of people without establishing a socially grounded public.

MF4 already distinguishes analyst-selected composition from target-system standing.

Therefore:

```text
GraphCommunityDetected
↛ SocialPublicStanding
```

unless the relation has grounded significance in the target social system/practice.

---

# 69. Network community detection ≠ public formation

A modularity algorithm can partition communication graphs into clusters.

These clusters may be useful evidence for publics but can also reflect:

```text
algorithm choice
sampling
platform affordances
language/geography
spam/bot behavior
```

Therefore:

```text
DetectedCluster
≠ Public by identity
```

Network remains an evidence/structure layer.

---

# 70. Public formation is not reducible to collective agency

Repeated because it is the most dangerous Host/Agency ownership collision:

```text
Public
```

can be internally conflictual, loosely bounded and non-agentic.

Do not force it into MF8 merely because it is collective.

MF8 provides agency only when collective-level source/evaluative/regulation/action standing exists.

---

# 71. Public formation is not reducible to Institution either

Not every public is a formal institution.

Issue publics and discourse publics can be emergent, transient or informal.

Therefore:

```text
PublicFormation
≠ Institution
```

But Institution often supplies:

```text
citizenship
rights
recognized membership
procedures
official forums
authority
representation mechanisms
```

for particular public routes.

Ownership is Human/social with institutional specializations, not Institution alone.

---

# 72. Human/social ontology is the likely primary owner

Across rival traditions, public formation requires some grounded social relation among/around bearers:

```text
affectedness or common concern
address/orientation
participation
belonging/identity
communicative practice
discourse circulation
social recognition
institutional role
```

These are not generic Media relations.

Media frequently provides the representations and mediation through which they become observable/maintainable.

Thus current ownership points to:

```text
PublicFormation → primarily Human/social
                   + Institution/Political specialization
                   + Media cross-cutting realization
```

# 74. Proposed `PublicFormationProfile`

Because rival public theories identify different formation routes, the profile must be plural/typed rather than presupposing one essence:

```text
PublicFormationProfile(P | Σ) = <
  PublicBearer/Formation Identifier,
```

# 91. Research anchors used

Representative comparison/falsification anchors:

- Jürgen Habermas public-sphere tradition, as summarized by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — private individuals form communicative public spheres around matters of general concern; public opinion formation is social/institutional rather than equivalent to open access or mass audience.
- John Dewey, *The Public and Its Problems* (1927) — publics can arise around indirect consequences of conjoint action and may remain latent/inchoate before effective organization.
- Michael Warner, *Publics and Counterpublics* (2002) — public formation through address to strangers and reflexive circulation of discourse; public is not simply a bounded membership group.
- Nancy Fraser counterpublic tradition — plural/subaltern counterpublics pressure one-unified-public and consensus assumptions.
- Axel Bruns, `From “the” public sphere to a network of publics` (Communication Theory, 2023) — contemporary public communication is better modeled as multiple intersecting publics; participants and circulating posts are jointly relevant.
- `Public connection repertoires and communicative figurations of publics` (Communication Theory, 2023) — actor constellations, frames of relevance and communicative practices; polity/topic/group publics demonstrate multiple formation routes.
- `Elements of practices in digital publics` (Communication Theory, 2025) — digital-public practices involve action, language, materiality, collectivity, knowledge and normativity rather than one audience-size property.
- Recent communicative-AI / AI-deliberation research (2025) as Agent-era pressure cases, not ontology authorities.
- MF0/MF2/MF3/MF4/MF6/MF7/MF8 frozen Ordivon foundations plus Round B-A/B-B/B-D/B-F/B-G/B-J.

These sources pressure rival definitions; none overrides Ordivon's first-principles irreducibility test.
