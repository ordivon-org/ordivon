# 1. Problem statement

MF4-A proposed:

`Composition = Plurality + Typed Constitutive Relations/Constraints + Whole-level Organization + Scope`.

But it exposed a hidden assumption:

`P = {p_1,...,p_n}`

was treated as if the parts were already given.

That assumption fails across perception, events, software, signals, maps and continuous fields.

MF4-B therefore asks:

> **What makes something a part/unit at all, where does its boundary come from, how does unit identity persist, and how can a system segment a continuous or ambiguous substrate without presupposing the units it is trying to discover?**

The main target is the naive picture:

`World → pre-given atoms → group atoms → whole`.

The stronger candidate will be reciprocal:

`substrate/evidence ↔ candidate boundaries ↔ unit hypotheses ↔ whole/context/task`.

---

# 2. First distinction — Part ≠ Region ≠ Unit ≠ Token ≠ Role

These terms are often collapsed.

## Part

Something that stands in a part–whole relation under a declared composition/scope.

## Region

A spatial, temporal, state-space or other delimited extent, whether or not treated as one functional/perceptual unit.

## Unit

A region/token/process/distinction treated as one jointly addressable, trackable, operable or evaluable entity under a system/question.

## Token

A particular occurrence/realization of a unit/type.

## Role

The position/function occupied by a unit within a larger organization.

### Result

**CB-01 — Part, region, unit, token and role are distinct. One physical extent can instantiate different units/roles under different organizations.**

---

# 3. Part is a relation, not an intrinsic label

Calling `x` a part is incomplete.

One must ask:

`PartOf(x, W | R, Σ)`

where:

- `W` = proposed whole;
- `R` = relevant composition relation;
- `Σ` = scope/granularity/context.

A screw is a part of a machine mechanically.

The same screw may not be a meaningful part in a visual-layout decomposition of the factory.

### Result

**CB-02 — Part status is relation-/whole-/scope-relative rather than a context-free intrinsic property.**

---

# 4. Whole-relative part identity does not imply arbitrary decomposition

If part status is relational, an analyst might choose arbitrary subsets.

Reject.

A composition-relevant part must be constrained by relations such as:

- physical discontinuity;
- continuity/cohesion;
- functional coupling;
- interface contract;
- perceptual grouping;
- tracking identity;
- schema/token rule;
- causal autonomy;
- temporal/event structure;
- conventional demarcation.

### Result

**CB-03 — Relational part status is constrained by organization/evidence, not arbitrary subset selection.**

---

# 5. Boundary ≠ Part

A boundary is not itself automatically the part it delimits.

The same boundary can:

- separate two units;
- belong preferentially to one unit;
- be shared;
- be purely fiat/conventional;
- be a transition zone rather than a crisp surface;
- be inferred rather than sensed locally.

### Result

**CB-04 — Boundary and bounded unit are distinct ontology roles.**

---

# 6. Boundary locus ≠ Boundary ownership

Visual figure–ground research provides a decisive case.

Zhou, Friedman & von der Heydt presented the same local edge while changing which side belonged to a figure. Neural responses in substantial fractions of V2/V4 cells depended on the side of figure ownership.

Thus the local edge location can remain constant while boundary assignment changes.

### Result

**CB-05 — Boundary location and boundary ownership/orientation are distinct. A boundary can be the same local discontinuity yet belong to different units under global organization.**

---

# 7. Boundary ≠ Local physical discontinuity

A visible luminance/color/depth discontinuity may indicate a boundary.

But not every discontinuity is an object/unit boundary:

- shadow edge;
- texture change;
- reflection;
- printed stripe;
- internal material change.

Conversely, a unit boundary may have no local visible discontinuity because of:

- occlusion;
- camouflage;
- conventional demarcation;
- inferred temporal/event transition;
- software/API module separation.

### Result

**CB-06 — Local discontinuity is neither universally necessary nor sufficient for unit boundaries.**

---

# 8. Bona fide vs fiat boundaries — useful but insufficient

Smith & Varzi distinguish **bona fide** boundaries grounded in physical/spatiotemporal discontinuity from **fiat** boundaries induced by demarcation, such as many geographic borders.

MF4-B retains this as one important axis.

But media/composition requires more than two categories because boundaries may be:

- perceptually inferred;
- functionally/interface-defined;
- representational/schema-defined;
- event/prediction-defined;
- probabilistic/graded.

### Result

**CB-07 — Bona fide/fiat is an important physical-demarcation distinction, not an exhaustive boundary ontology.**

---

# 9. Provisional boundary-type profile

MF4-B distinguishes at least:

## B0 — Physical/material boundary

Surface/discontinuity/cohesion change in the substrate.

## B1 — Topological/geometric boundary

Extremal/closure/contact boundary under a spatial formalism.

## B2 — Perceptual boundary

A system's inferred/organized separation between perceptual units/surfaces/streams/events.

## B3 — Ownership boundary

Boundary assignment to a particular side/unit/figure/source.

## B4 — Functional boundary

A division defined by causal/function/control responsibility.

## B5 — Interface boundary

An engineered contract separating partially autonomous components.

## B6 — Representational/token boundary

A code/schema/syntax/convention that determines where one representational unit ends and another begins.

## B7 — Temporal/event boundary

A transition that segments ongoing activity/process into event units.

## B8 — Social/institutional/fiat boundary

A conventionally instituted demarcation not necessarily aligned with physical discontinuity.

## B9 — Epistemic/uncertain boundary

A probability/distribution over possible delimitations rather than one fixed cut.

These may overlap in one case.

### Result

**CB-08 — Boundary should be modeled as a typed profile, not one universal geometric primitive.**

---

# 10. Boundary locus may be thick/graded

Real transitions can occupy zones rather than zero-thickness surfaces:

- cloud edge;
- coastline under tides;
- biological tissue transition;
- crossfade;
- vague semantic category;
- event transition period.

### Result

**CB-09 — Crisp codimension-one boundaries are not universally required; transition regions and graded boundary confidence are admissible.**

---

# 11. Unit formation ≠ Grouping

Palmer & Rock's uniform-connectedness framework explicitly separates early unit formation from later grouping/parsing.

A closed homogeneous region tends to be treated as an initial unit, after which proximity/similarity and other relations can group units.

### Result

**CB-10 — Unit formation and grouping are distinct stages/relations; grouping presupposes candidate units, but candidate units can later be revised by grouping/context.**

---

# 12. Uniform connectedness is strong but not universal

Uniform connectedness can dominate proximity/similarity in many visual displays.

Yet Palmer & Rock themselves note cases where perceived depth/organization changes whether retinally connected regions count as one unit.

### Result

**CB-11 — Uniform connectedness is a strong perceptual unitization cue, not a universal unit criterion.**

---

# 13. Unitization and grouping can be reciprocal

The naive serial pipeline:

`segment first → group later`

fails when higher-order interpretation changes segmentation.

Examples:

- occlusion reveals that visible fragments belong to one hidden object;
- text context determines whether marks form one character or several;
- scene context affects border ownership;
- software responsibility determines module boundaries.

### Result

**CB-12 — Segmentation and grouping/whole inference can be recurrent and mutually constraining rather than strictly feed-forward.**

---

# 14. Occlusion is a decisive falsifier of visible-region = unit

A partially hidden object appears as several disconnected visible regions.

If visible connected component were the object unit, one object would become several whenever occluded.

Humans instead often perceive one continuing object behind the occluder.

### Result

**CB-13 — Visible connected regions need not equal perceived/object units; occlusion can split evidence without splitting unit identity.**

---

# 15. Amodal completion falsifies boundary = sensed contour

Kellman & Shipley develop a theory in which partially occluded objects and illusory figures involve boundary interpolation across locally unspecified regions.

The system infers continuation from relations among visible contour fragments and spatiotemporal information.

### Result

**CB-14 — Unit boundaries can be inferred/completed across missing local evidence; boundary standing can exceed currently sensed contours.**

---

# 16. Boundary interpolation is not arbitrary completion

Kellman & Shipley's `relatability` constrains which contour fragments support smooth monotonic interpolation.

Later work shows no single contour rule is universally necessary/sufficient, but the broader lesson survives:

> completion is constrained by global shape/depth/surface organization.

### Result

**CB-15 — Amodal completion demonstrates model-/constraint-guided boundary inference, not unconstrained hallucination.**

---

# 17. Intrinsic vs extrinsic contours

Nakayama, Shimojo & Silverman distinguish:

- **intrinsic contours** belonging to an object's own shape;
- **extrinsic contours** produced by occlusion relations and belonging to the nearer occluder rather than the hidden object's true shape.

### Result

**CB-16 — A visible contour can delimit the current image region without belonging to the represented object's own boundary. Image-region boundary ≠ object boundary.**

---

# 18. Border ownership makes boundary asymmetric

At figure–ground borders, a shared image edge can be assigned to one side as figure.

Thus a boundary relation may encode:

`BoundaryOf(b, A)`

without symmetrically implying the same boundary role for B.

### Result

**CB-17 — Boundary relations can be oriented/asymmetric through ownership; separation alone is too weak.**

---

# 19. Object file evidence — identity persists through changing features

Kahneman, Treisman & Gibbs found object-specific preview benefits when successive displays were linked as states of the same object via location/motion-frame continuity.

They proposed temporary episodic `object files` integrating successive states.

### Result

**CB-18 — Unit identity can persist through changes of local features and position when spatiotemporal/object continuity supports one token history.**

---

# 20. Unit identity ≠ Feature identity

An object can change:

- color;
- location;
- orientation;
- visible extent;

without becoming a new object token.

Conversely two visually identical objects can remain distinct tokens.

### Result

**CB-19 — Unit/token identity and current feature-state identity are distinct.**

---

# 21. Tracking provides a token-level identity relation

Pylyshyn & Storm show observers can track multiple moving targets among identical distractors, supporting a mechanism that maintains several individuated dynamic loci/tokens without requiring unique descriptive features for each.

### Result

**CB-20 — Dynamic unit individuation can depend on continuity/indexing rather than stable descriptive feature labels.**

---

# 22. Tracking is not full object representation

Tracking a token does not imply representation of all its properties.

A system may preserve:

- identity/index;

while poorly preserving:

- precise position;
- detailed features;
- semantic class.

### Result

**CB-21 — Individuation/tracking identity and rich object representation are distinct capabilities.**

---

# 23. Identity switch is a distinct segmentation/tracking failure

If tracker A begins following object B while preserving one continuous track ID, the boundary/segmentation may be locally correct but token identity becomes wrong.

### Result

**CB-22 — Identity switch is distinct from merge/split/boundary-location error.**

---

# 24. Object is not the universal unit

MF2 already rejected object-centrism.

MF4-B must admit units such as:

- event;
- stream;
- surface;
- field;
- texture region;
- body state;
- relation;
- phrase;
- module;
- conversation turn;
- process phase.

### Result

**CB-23 — Unit ontology must be typed; `object` is one unit profile, not the universal atom of composition.**

---

# 25. Surface units differ from object units

A visible surface patch can be segmented as one region while belonging to a larger object.

Conversely one object can expose multiple surfaces with different orientations/materials.

### Result

**CB-24 — Surface segmentation and object segmentation are distinct levels.**

---

# 26. Stream units differ from source objects

Auditory stream segregation provides a strong case.

Bregman & Campbell showed rapid sequences of high/low tones can perceptually segregate into separate streams, changing which temporal orders listeners can perceive.

The physical acoustic sequence is fixed, yet organization into streams changes functional/perceptual unit structure.

### Result

**CB-25 — Auditory/perceptual stream units are emergent organizational tracks, not identical to raw temporal adjacency or physical sample boundaries.**

---

# 27. One source can yield multiple streams; multiple sources can yield one perceptual stream

Depending on cues/rate/context, perceptual organization can split or fuse acoustic evidence.

### Result

**CB-26 — Source identity, signal channel identity and perceptual stream identity are distinct.**

---

# 28. Temporal segmentation needs event units

Continuous activity has no universal set of obvious physical cut points.

Zacks et al. show observers reliably segment everyday activity into meaningful temporal units; boundaries correlate with salient changes and neural activity.

### Result

**CB-27 — Event boundaries are perceptual/cognitive organization of continuous activity, not merely moments when physical input stops.**

---

# 29. Event boundaries are hierarchical

Observers segment activity into coarse and fine units.

The same instant may be:

- inside a coarse event;
- boundary between fine subevents.

### Result

**CB-28 — Temporal boundaries are granularity-relative and can be nested hierarchically.**

---

# 30. Event segmentation combines bottom-up and top-down constraints

Zacks & Swallow summarize evidence that segmentation depends on sensory change such as movement and conceptual structure such as actors' goals.

### Result

**CB-29 — Event segmentation is neither purely sensory-discontinuity detection nor purely conceptual parsing; multiple constraint levels interact.**

---

# 31. Prediction change is a strong event-boundary mechanism candidate

Event Segmentation Theory proposes that perceptual systems maintain event models to predict near-future activity and update them when prediction becomes less accurate.

MF4-B does not freeze this as the universal definition of event boundaries.

### Result

**CB-30 — Prediction error/model updating is a strong mechanism hypothesis for many event boundaries, not a universal boundary ontology.**

---

# 32. Event boundary ≠ State change

Every microstate change cannot define a new event, otherwise continuous dynamics yield infinitely dense boundaries.

Relevant transitions depend on scale, predictability, goals and organization.

### Result

**CB-31 — Change is necessary for many temporal boundaries but not every change is event-defining; event segmentation is scale/relevance constrained.**

---

# 33. Event identity can persist across brief interruption

An action temporarily occluded or interrupted may still be understood as the same event if causal/goal/predictive continuity survives.

### Result

**CB-32 — Temporal unit identity can bridge evidence gaps; temporal contiguity is not universally necessary.**

---

# 34. Boundary evidence can be modality-specific

A visual cut, audio silence and action-goal change need not coincide.

### Result

**CB-33 — Cross-modal event boundaries can be asynchronous; multimodal segmentation must not assume one universal boundary timestamp.**

---

# 35. Representational tokenization is not perceptual segmentation

A tokenizer may split:

`unbelievable`

into arbitrary computational subword units.

The perceptual/language user may treat the word/morphemes differently.

### Result

**CB-34 — Representational/code token boundaries and perceptual/semantic unit boundaries are distinct.**

---

# 36. Delimiters do not guarantee semantic units

Whitespace, commas, packet boundaries and markup tags define formal/token boundaries.

They may or may not align with semantic units.

### Result

**CB-35 — Formal delimiter/schema boundary establishes code structure, not automatically semantic part identity.**

---

# 37. No-delimiter representations can still have units

Continuous images, waveforms and vector fields lack explicit separators but can be segmented into regions/streams/events by system organization.

### Result

**CB-36 — Explicit discrete delimiters are not necessary for unitization.**

---

# 38. Continuous fields require region-based unitization

For a continuous field `F(x,t)`, candidate units may be induced by:

- level sets;
- discontinuities;
- gradients;
- coherence;
- topology;
- dynamics;
- task-relevant regions.

### Result

**CB-37 — Composition ontology must support region/field units without pre-given atomic tokens.**

---

# 39. Segmentation is a partition only in special cases

A mathematical partition requires disjoint exhaustive subsets.

But real segmentation may involve:

- overlap;
- uncertain membership;
- background/unassigned regions;
- nested units;
- multiple simultaneous segmentations.

### Result

**CB-38 — `Segmentation = partition` is too strong as a universal definition.**

---

# 40. Soft segmentation

A system may assign membership weights:

`P(unit=k | evidence)`

or fuzzy degrees rather than hard labels.

### Result

**CB-39 — Unit membership/boundaries can be probabilistic/graded.**

---

# 41. Boundary uncertainty ≠ Unit uncertainty

A system can be confident that one object exists while uncertain about its exact edge under blur/occlusion.

Conversely boundary location may be crisp while unit category/identity is uncertain.

### Result

**CB-40 — Boundary confidence and unit-identity confidence are separate uncertainty dimensions.**

---

# 42. Boundary precision ≠ Boundary correctness

A highly precise contour can be attached to the wrong object/surface.

### Result

**CB-41 — Boundary localization accuracy and ownership/semantic correctness are distinct.**

---

# 43. Boundary persistence through transformation

A unit may preserve identity while its boundary changes continuously:

- deforming object;
- moving cloud;
- growing organism;
- resizable UI window.

### Result

**CB-42 — Unit persistence does not require boundary-shape invariance.**

---

# 44. Boundary persistence can fail while material persists

A software refactor can redefine module boundaries while code lines remain.

A political border can change while terrain remains.

### Result

**CB-43 — Boundary identity can change without substrate destruction.**

---

# 45. Functional boundaries can cross physical boundaries

A distributed service may be one functional module across multiple machines.

Conversely one process/container may host several logically separate modules.

### Result

**CB-44 — Functional/module boundaries and physical deployment boundaries are independent axes.**

---

# 46. Parnas is a hard software-boundary falsifier

Parnas compares decompositions of the same software system and argues that modularization quality depends on the criterion used to divide the system, especially information-hiding/design-decision responsibilities.

The same program behavior can therefore support materially different module boundaries.

### Result

**CB-45 — Engineered unit boundaries can be selected by change responsibility/interface/information-hiding criteria rather than physical or execution contiguity.**

---

# 47. Interface is a boundary specification, not the whole component

An API/interface states what crosses a boundary:

- operations;
- data/schema;
- timing;
- errors;
- ownership;
- invariants.

The implementation behind it may change.

### Result

**CB-46 — Interface boundary and component implementation are distinct; stable boundaries can support replaceable interiors.**

---

# 48. Encapsulation creates selective permeability

A useful boundary often does not isolate completely.

It controls which relations can cross.

### Result

**CB-47 — Functional boundaries are frequently selectively permeable rather than absolute separations.**

---

# 49. Boundary crossing is typed

Across a boundary may pass:

- matter;
- energy;
- information;
- control;
- references;
- rights/authority;
- signals;
- attention.

### Result

**CB-48 — `Boundary permeability` is multidimensional; crossing in one relation does not eliminate separation in another.**

---

# 50. Social/institutional boundaries can be causal despite being fiat

A jurisdictional line can alter legal/administrative consequences even without a physical discontinuity.

### Result

**CB-49 — Fiat/conventional boundaries can have real downstream causal/normative effects; nonphysical origin does not imply irrelevance.**

---

# 51. Representational boundaries can create operational parts

A JSON object, AST node, database row or document block is delimited by schema/syntax.

Consumers can address/manipulate these as units.

### Result

**CB-50 — Code/schema boundaries can constitute operational representational units even when their physical bytes are contiguous with other units.**

---

# 52. Unit identity can be content-addressed, location-addressed or history-tracked

Possible identity schemes:

- same location;
- same persistent ID;
- same content digest;
- same causal trajectory;
- same role/history;
- same object file/index.

### Result

**CB-51 — Unit identity requires a typed persistence criterion; there is no universal identity key.**

---

# 53. Same content ≠ Same token

Two files can contain identical bytes yet be distinct artifact tokens/provenance histories.

Two visually identical objects remain different tracked tokens.

### Result

**CB-52 — Type/content equivalence does not collapse token individuation.**

---

# 54. Same token can change content/role

A mutable database row keeps one ID while fields change.

A tracked visual object changes color.

### Result

**CB-53 — Token continuity can survive state/content change.**

---

# 55. Merge operation

Two candidate units become one unit hypothesis.

Possible triggers:

- evidence of common motion/source;
- discovered shared identity;
- schema grouping;
- functional refactor.

### Result

**CB-54 — Merge is an explicit unitization operation, not merely deletion of a boundary.**

---

# 56. Split operation

One candidate unit becomes multiple units.

Possible triggers:

- revealed independent motion;
- source separation;
- new functional responsibilities;
- semantic parsing.

### Result

**CB-55 — Split is an explicit re-individuation operation and can preserve relations/provenance from the parent unit.**

---

# 57. Merge/split can be reversible or irreversible

Segmentation history may matter.

A system that merged two tracks may lose enough identity evidence that later split cannot recover which was which.

### Result

**CB-56 — Unitization operations can be history-dependent and noninvertible.**

---

# 58. Segmentation error taxonomy

MF4-B proposes:

## Over-segmentation / Split error

One intended/relevant unit represented as multiple units.

## Under-segmentation / Merge error

Multiple relevant units collapsed into one.

## Boundary displacement

Right units, wrong boundary locus.

## Boundary ownership error

Right locus, assigned to wrong side/unit.

## Identity switch

Track/unit IDs exchange across time.

## Fragmentation

One persistent unit repeatedly loses/reacquires identity.

## Spurious unit

Unit inferred without sufficient relevant organization.

## Missed unit

Relevant unit not instantiated.

## Granularity mismatch

Segmentation too coarse/fine for the task/scope.

## Temporal boundary timing error

Event change detected too early/late.

### Result

**CB-57 — Segmentation error is a typed family, not one IoU-like scalar defect.**

---

# 59. Boundary metrics are task-dependent

Pixel IoU can score a visual segmentation while missing:

- object identity;
- topology;
- ownership;
- temporal continuity;
- functional relevance.

### Result

**CB-58 — Boundary/segmentation quality requires typed metrics matched to unit ontology and downstream use.**

---

# 60. Granularity is first-class

The same scene can be segmented into:

- pixels;
- edges;
- surface patches;
- objects;
- groups;
- scene regions.

The same activity can be segmented into:

- micro-actions;
- actions;
- episodes;
- projects.

### Result

**CB-59 — Segmentation always requires an explicit or implicit granularity/scale profile.**

---

# 61. Fine segmentation is not universally better

Overly fine units increase:

- coordination cost;
- identity burden;
- noise sensitivity;
- complexity.

### Result

**CB-60 — More parts/finer boundaries are not universally more accurate/useful; granularity trades retained distinctions against tractability and task sufficiency.**

---

# 62. Coarse segmentation is not universally worse

A macro-event may be the correct unit for planning even if it collapses micro-actions.

A software module intentionally hides internal routines.

### Result

**CB-61 — Coarse units can be normatively superior at a declared scope; boundary evaluation is scope-relative.**

---

# 63. Multiple granularities can coexist simultaneously

A system can maintain:

- object + part hierarchy;
- fine + coarse event models;
- package + module + function boundaries.

### Result

**CB-62 — Unitization need not choose one globally exclusive granularity.**

---

# 64. Boundary hierarchy ≠ Part hierarchy

A boundary at one level can cut across units at another level.

Example:

- administrative district crosses ecological region;
- functional subsystem spans machines;
- sentence prosody boundary differs from syntactic boundary.

### Result

**CB-63 — Different boundary systems can cross-cut; one master hierarchy is not universal.**

---

# 65. Units can overlap

Examples:

- overlapping social groups;
- software concerns/aspects;
- visual transparent surfaces;
- linguistic constructions;
- functional neural assemblies.

### Result

**CB-64 — Unit membership need not be disjoint. Overlap is a legitimate composition relation.**

---

# 66. Units can be nested without ownership identity

A wheel is part of a bicycle, but its boundary remains physically distinct.

A clause is part of a sentence while retaining token identity.

### Result

**CB-65 — Nested part–whole relation does not imply dissolution of subunit identity.**

---

# 67. Boundary can be active process

In dynamic systems, maintaining a boundary can require work:

- cell membrane transport;
- firewall/filter;
- access-control layer;
- active attention mask;
- homeostatic control.

### Result

**CB-66 — Some boundaries are dynamically maintained processes rather than static geometric delimiters.**

---

# 68. Boundary can be specification without current process

A firewall rule, schema or legal border can have standing even when no packet/person currently crosses it.

### Result

**CB-67 — Boundary standing and active enforcement are distinct.**

---

# 69. Boundary detection ≠ Boundary creation

A sensor can detect a pre-existing physical edge.

A legislature can create a fiat jurisdictional boundary.

A parser can instantiate token boundaries under a grammar.

A perceptual system can infer an occluded boundary.

### Result

**CB-68 — Boundary provenance includes discovery, inference, specification and institution; these are distinct acts.**

---

# 70. Segmentation evidence ≠ Segmentation ontology

An edge detector, clustering algorithm or human judgment provides evidence/procedure.

It does not define what the unit ontologically is.

### Result

**CB-69 — Boundary/segmentation algorithms and the units they aim to identify must remain conceptually separate.**

---

# 71. Unitization is often inference under uncertainty

Given evidence `E`, system may compare candidate unit structures:

`P(U,B | E,C,T)`.

### Result

**CB-70 — Segmentation is often hypothesis selection/inference over unit-boundary structures, not deterministic edge extraction.**

---

# 72. Unitization can be action-coupled

A system may move viewpoint, touch an object, query a service or inspect metadata to resolve ambiguous boundaries.

### Result

**CB-71 — Boundary discovery can be active; actions can be selected to disambiguate unit structure.**

This connects to MF2 active perception.

---

# 73. Resource constraints shape segmentation

Finite attention/computation may force a system to maintain fewer/coarser units.

### Result

**CB-72 — Unitization is often resource-bounded; the maintained segmentation can be a task-dependent quotient of finer possible structure.**

---

# 74. Segmentation can be learned

Systems can learn which discontinuities/relations predict useful stable units.

### Result

**CB-73 — Unit boundaries need not be innately fixed; learned priors/history can shape segmentation without making it arbitrary.**

---

# 75. Learned segmentation can be wrong

Shortcut cues can produce stable but misleading boundaries.

### Result

**CB-74 — Learned unitization requires falsification against causal/persistence/task structure, not just reproducibility.**

---

# 76. Unitization can be conventional

Words, records, legal parcels, chapters and API resources can be units because practices/specifications establish boundaries.

### Result

**CB-75 — Conventional/unit-specification boundaries are genuine organizational boundaries even when not grounded in physical discontinuities.**

---

# 77. Unitization can be operational

A unit can be defined by what can be independently:

- addressed;
- moved;
- replaced;
- queried;
- updated;
- evaluated;
- tracked.

### Result

**CB-76 — Operational addressability is strong evidence for unit status, though not universally necessary.**

---

# 78. Unit autonomy is graded

A component can have varying degrees of:

- state independence;
- causal autonomy;
- replaceability;
- interface closure;
- persistence.

### Result

**CB-77 — Unit autonomy is a profile, not a binary property.**

---

# 79. Boundary strength is multidimensional

Possible dimensions:

- physical discontinuity;
- topological closure;
- perceptual salience;
- ownership confidence;
- causal insulation;
- interface restriction;
- semantic/token delimiter strength;
- temporal change magnitude;
- conventional authority.

### Result

**CB-78 — Boundary strength is typed/multidimensional; no universal scalar boundary strength is assumed.**

---

# 80. Unit persistence profile

A unit may persist by:

- material continuity;
- spatiotemporal trajectory;
- causal continuity;
- persistent identifier;
- role continuity;
- structural isomorphism;
- public convention;
- functional replacement continuity.

### Result

**CB-79 — Persistence criteria are unit-type specific and can disagree.**

---

# 81. Replacement reveals identity criteria

A server instance can be replaced while service identity remains.

An original artwork cannot always be replaced by an identical copy without losing token/provenance identity.

### Result

**CB-80 — Replacement tests expose which identity dimensions define the maintained whole/unit under scope.**

---

# 82. Unit boundaries can be relative to query

A database query may treat joined rows as one logical record.

A renderer treats primitives differently.

### Result

**CB-81 — Query/consumer can induce temporary operational units without erasing standing lower-level units.**

---

# 83. Temporary coalitions

Agents/services/features can form one unit only for a task episode.

### Result

**CB-82 — Units can be transient coalitions defined by temporary coupling/goal relations.**

---

# 84. One unit can have multiple boundary representations

A geographic country can be represented as:

- legal polygon;
- coastline geometry;
- customs-control zone;
- map raster mask.

### Result

**CB-83 — Boundary representation and bounded entity are distinct; one unit can have multiple operational boundary models.**

# 85. Boundary representation can be wrong while unit exists

A map can misdraw the border without changing the actual institutional boundary.

### Result

**CB-84 — Boundary representation error is distinct from boundary change.**

---

# 86. Segmentation and representation remain non-identical

A visual system can segment figure/ground before attributing rich semantic representation.

A parser can segment bytes into tokens without grounding their meaning.

### Result

**CB-85 — Segmentation/unitization is broader than semantic representation.**

---

# 87. Segmentation and signal remain non-identical

Signal processing can detect change points/frames without those units corresponding to perceptual events or represented objects.

### Result

**CB-86 — Signal segmentation is one layer; perceptual/representational unitization requires additional organization.**

---

# 88. Segmentation and composition are reciprocal

MF4-A initially made segmentation look prior to composition.

MF4-B revises that.

Candidate composition/whole hypotheses can determine which boundaries/parts are likely, while candidate boundaries constrain wholes.

### Result

**CB-87 — Segmentation and composition are mutually constraining inference/organization problems rather than a universal strict pipeline.**

# 92. Provisional minimal unit definition

> **A unit is a scope-relative individuated component/region/process/role whose internal distinctions are treated as sufficiently jointly organized and whose boundary/identity relations are sufficiently stable or specified for the relevant system/practice to address, track, compose, operate on or evaluate it as one.**

Important:

- not necessarily physically connected;
- not necessarily permanent;
- not necessarily object-like;
- not necessarily semantically represented;
- not necessarily crisp;
- can overlap with other units;
- can be whole at one scale and part at another.

---

# 93. Provisional minimal boundary definition

> **A boundary is a scope-relative delimiting/transition/ownership/interface relation that constrains which distinctions, regions, states or processes count as inside/outside, same/different unit, or permitted crossing for some unitization/composition profile.**

This is intentionally broader than geometric surface.

---

# 94. Provisional segmentation definition

> **Segmentation is the process or standing organization by which a substrate/evidence domain is structured into candidate units and boundaries at one or more granularities under typed physical, perceptual, functional, representational, temporal or conventional constraints.**

Compact:

`Segmentation ≠ Edge Detection ≠ Partition`.

# 97. Primary/original literature anchors

- Smith, B. & Varzi, A. C. (2000), `Fiat and Bona Fide Boundaries`, *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 60(2), 401–420. DOI: 10.2307/2653492. Distinguishes boundaries grounded in physical/spatiotemporal discontinuity from fiat human demarcations; MF4-B retains this as one boundary axis rather than an exhaustive typology.
- Palmer, S. E. & Rock, I. (1994), `Rethinking Perceptual Organization: The Role of Uniform Connectedness`, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 1(1), 29–55. DOI: 10.3758/BF03200760. Uniform connectedness as a strong entry-level perceptual unit principle while separating unit formation/grouping/parsing and discussing depth exceptions.
- Kellman, P. J. & Shipley, T. F. (1991), `A Theory of Visual Interpolation in Object Perception`, *Cognitive Psychology* 23(2), 141–221. DOI: 10.1016/0010-0285(91)90009-D. Develops boundary interpolation/unit-formation account for occluded objects and illusory figures.
- Nakayama, K., Shimojo, S. & Silverman, G. H. (1989), `Stereoscopic Depth: Its Relation to Image Segmentation, Grouping, and the Recognition of Occluded Objects`, *Perception* 18(1), 55–68. DOI: 10.1068/p180055. Distinguishes intrinsic object contours from extrinsic occlusion contours and emphasizes depth/border assignment.
- Zhou, H., Friedman, H. S. & von der Heydt, R. (2000), `Coding of Border Ownership in Monkey Visual Cortex`, *Journal of Neuroscience* 20(17), 6594–6611. DOI: 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.20-17-06594.2000. Same local edge produces different neural modulation depending on which side belongs to the figure, supporting boundary-ownership/context distinction.
- Kahneman, D., Treisman, A. & Gibbs, B. J. (1992), `The Reviewing of Object Files: Object-Specific Integration of Information`, *Cognitive Psychology* 24(2), 175–219. DOI: 10.1016/0010-0285(92)90007-O. Object-specific preview effects support temporary object-file identity linking successive states across motion/location changes.
- Pylyshyn, Z. W. & Storm, R. W. (1988), `Tracking Multiple Independent Targets: Evidence for a Parallel Tracking Mechanism`, *Spatial Vision* 3(3), 179–197. DOI: 10.1163/156856888X00122. Multiple dynamic tokens can be indexed/tracked among identical distractors.
- Bregman, A. S. & Campbell, J. (1971), `Primary Auditory Stream Segregation and Perception of Order in Rapid Sequences of Tones`, *Journal of Experimental Psychology* 89(2), 244–249. DOI: 10.1037/h0031163. Auditory stream organization changes perceived temporal relations despite one physical tone sequence.
- Zacks, J. M. et al. (2001), `Human Brain Activity Time-Locked to Perceptual Event Boundaries`, *Nature Neuroscience* 4, 651–655. DOI: 10.1038/88486. Everyday activity is segmented into meaningful temporal parts/subparts and neural activity aligns with perceptually salient event boundaries.
- Zacks, J. M. & Swallow, K. M. (2007), `Event Segmentation`, *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 16(2), 80–84. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-8721.2007.00480.x. Summarizes hierarchical event segmentation and combined bottom-up movement/top-down goal effects.

Proposes prediction/event-model updating account for event-boundary perception; retained as a strong mechanism hypothesis rather than boundary definition.
- Parnas, D. L. (1972), `On the Criteria To Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules`, *Communications of the ACM* 15(12), 1053–1058. DOI: 10.1145/361598.361623. Demonstrates that module boundaries depend on decomposition criteria such as hidden design decisions rather than being determined uniquely by the executable artifact.

# 98. Deep reconstruction

Before MF4-B the tempting ontology was:

```text
World/Substrate
   ↓ detect boundaries
Parts
   ↓ compose
Whole
```

MF4-B rejects this as universal.

The stronger candidate is:

```text
Substrate / evidence / current state
        │
        ├── discontinuities / continuity
        ├── motion / common fate
        ├── depth / occlusion
        ├── schema / convention
        ├── causal coupling / interfaces
        ├── prediction / goals
        └── prior/history/task
        │
        ▼
Candidate boundary hypotheses
        ⇅
Candidate unit identities / tracks
        ⇅
Grouping / whole / composition hypotheses
        ⇅
Action / additional evidence / context
        │
        ▼
Maintained multi-scale unit-boundary organization
```

No layer is assumed universally prior to all others.
