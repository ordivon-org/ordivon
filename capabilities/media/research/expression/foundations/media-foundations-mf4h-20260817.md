# 1. Problem statement

MF4-A→G established composition across units, relations, hierarchy, time, space and heterogeneous modalities. Yet many media wholes are not exhausted by those structures.

A scene is not merely the union of visible objects.
A montage is not merely several shots placed in sequence.
A narrative is not merely chronologically ordered events.
A discourse is not merely grammatically valid sentences.

The target question is:

> **What additional organization turns objects/regions/events/shots/utterances into a situation, scene, montage, narrative or coherent discourse whole?**

The dangerous collapses are:

`Object Inventory = Scene`;

`Scene = Image`;

`Shot = Scene = Event = Episode`;

`Shot Sequence = Event Sequence = Narrative`;

`Chronology = Presentation Order = Discourse Order = Causal Order`;

`Context = Background`;

`Coherence = Consistency = Agreement = Continuity`;

`Narrative = Causal Chain`;

`Discourse = Sentence Sequence`;

`Scene Graph = Scene`;

`Omitted Event = Nonexistent Event`;

`Inferred Event = Observed Event`.

MF4-H rejects all of these as universal identities.

# 2. Object inventory is insufficient for scene identity

Two scenes can contain the same object categories but differ in:

- spatial layout;
- environmental affordances;
- background field;
- relations;
- event state;
- viewpoint;
- functional context.

### Result

**CH-01 — Scene identity cannot be reduced to the set/multiset of recognized objects.**

---

# 3. Scene can carry global structure before complete object inventory

Greene & Oliva's rapid-scene work shows human scene categorization can be strongly predicted by global properties such as openness, navigability, mean depth and expansion; object-centered models tested in that work had lower fidelity to human error patterns than the global-property model.

### Result

**CH-02 — Scene-level organization can be available/useful without prior exhaustive object recognition.**

---

# 4. Scene-level property ≠ One object property

`navigable`, `open`, `enclosed`, `deep`, `cluttered` and similar scene properties depend on spatial relations/fields across regions.

### Result

**CH-03 — Scene properties can be relational/global properties of layout/field organization rather than attributes of one constituent object.**

---

# 5. Global property ≠ Complete scene understanding

Scene category/gist does not specify every object, action, causal relation or hidden state.

### Result

**CH-04 — Rapid scene gist/global structure is weaker than full scene model.**

---

# 6. Scene gist ≠ Object blindness

Fei-Fei et al.'s brief-glance reports show both scene- and object-level information can become available rapidly; there is no universal requirement that only one level exists first.

### Result

**CH-05 — Global and local/object scene information can coexist; scene-centered evidence does not entail object irrelevance.**

# 148. Scene/narrative failure taxonomy

## Object-inventory fallacy

Scene reduced to detected objects while layout/global/field relations omitted.

## Scene/shot collapse

Edit unit mistaken for represented situation unit.

## Event/scene collapse

Event boundaries mistaken for location/shot boundaries.

## Chronology/discourse confusion

Presentation order mistaken for represented event order.

## False causal link

Juxtaposition/sequence wrongly interpreted as causation.

## Missing causal bridge

Required relation for coherence omitted.

## Inference provenance loss

Inferred event remembered/reported as directly presented.

## Ellipsis/loss confusion

Editorial omission mistaken for data corruption or vice versa.

## Coreference error

Mention assigned to wrong discourse entity.

## Agent/goal misbinding

Action/intention assigned to wrong agent/plan.

## Epistemic-scope error

Belief/report/dream/hypothesis treated as world fact.

## Viewpoint/focalization error

Information access/source perspective misassigned.

## Local/global coherence mismatch

Smooth local transitions fail global structure or abrupt local transition wrongly judged globally incoherent.

## Scene-graph overclaim

Formal object-relation encoding treated as complete scene/world model.

## Cross-modal narrative misbinding

Narration/music/caption assigned wrong role/source/event.

## Narrative compression loss

Abridgment removes composition-defining event/relation.

## Story/discourse identity confusion

Core event structure and its realization/version treated as identical.

## Intended/perceived divergence

Consumer interpretation differs from encoded/designed relation structure.

### Result

**CH-147 — Scene/narrative failure is a typed relational family, not one sequence-level score.**

# 157. Primary/original literature anchors

- Greene, M. R. & Oliva, A. (2009), `Recognition of natural scenes from global properties: Seeing the forest without representing the trees`, *Cognitive Psychology* 58(2), 137–176. DOI: 10.1016/j.cogpsych.2008.06.001. Human rapid categorization/error patterns were strongly predicted by global scene properties; object-centered models tested had lower behavioral fidelity. Used as a falsifier of `scene = object inventory`, not proof that objects are unnecessary for all scene understanding.
- Fei-Fei, L., Iyer, A., Koch, C. & Perona, P. (2007), `What do we perceive in a glance of a real-world scene?`, *Journal of Vision* 7(1):10. DOI: 10.1167/7.1.10. Free-report brief-glance work shows rich scene/object information can coexist rapidly and prevents an overcorrection to `global only`.
- Mobbs, D., Weiskopf, N., Lau, H. C., Featherstone, E., Dolan, R. J. & Frith, C. D. (2006), `The Kuleshov Effect: the influence of contextual framing on emotional attributions`, *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* 1(2), 95–106. DOI: 10.1093/scan/nsl014. Identical faces in different contextual movie frames received different expression/mental-state attributions, a controlled montage/context falsifier.
- Trabasso, T. & van den Broek, P. (1985), `Causal thinking and the representation of narrative events`, *Journal of Memory and Language* 24(5), 612–630. DOI: 10.1016/0749-596X(85)90049-X. Causal-chain membership/connectivity predicted recall, summarization and judged importance, supporting causal organization beyond serial position.
- Zwaan, R. A., Langston, M. C. & Graesser, A. C. (1995), `The Construction of Situation Models in Narrative Comprehension: An Event-Indexing Model`, *Psychological Science* 6(5), 292–297. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00513.x. Narrative events related along time, space, protagonist/entity, causality and intentionality dimensions; used as evidence for multidimensional situation organization rather than universal event ontology.
- Grosz, B. J., Joshi, A. K. & Weinstein, S. (1995), `Centering: A Framework for Modeling the Local Coherence of Discourse`, *Computational Linguistics* 21(2), 203–225. Provides a formal account of local discourse coherence through discourse-entity/center transitions; used to separate local entity continuity from global narrative coherence.
- Mann, W. C. & Thompson, S. A. (1988), `Rhetorical Structure Theory: Toward a functional theory of text organization`, *Text* 8(3), 243–281. DOI: 10.1515/text.1.1988.8.3.243. Typed functional relations among text spans demonstrate discourse organization beyond sentence adjacency/grammar; retained as one discourse formalism, not universal narrative ontology.

MF4-H replaces it with:

# 159. Deepest MF4-H conclusion

The strongest surviving candidate is:

> **A scene/situation composition exists when objects, regions, fields, events and contextual conditions participate in global spatial-functional-event relations that support a whole-level situation identity, affordance, interpretation or state beyond their inventory.**

And:

> **A narrative/discourse composition exists when represented events/states and presentation segments are organized through typed temporal, causal, intentional, participant, rhetorical, modal, viewpoint and information-release relations such that the whole supports structured interpretation across omissions, reordering, inference and multiple scales.**

Critically:

> **Narrative coherence is not agreement or continuity; it is a profile of intelligible/valid relations under scope, and it can include conflict, ellipsis, uncertainty and deliberate discontinuity.**
