# 1. Problem statement

MF0–MF3 established several non-collapsed primitives:

- structured mediation;
- active discriminative variation;
- perceptual organization under uncertainty;
- grounded representational standing.

None yet explains a more general phenomenon:

> **How do multiple distinctions, parts, states, events, signals, percepts or representations become organized as a larger whole rather than merely coexisting?**

The word `composition` is dangerously overloaded.

It can mean:

- mereological fusion;
- physical assembly;
- perceptual grouping;
- feature binding;
- temporal sequencing;
- synchronization;
- signal multiplexing;
- symbolic syntax;
- model assembly;
- multimodal integration;
- layout;
- montage;
- narrative ordering;
- interactive coordination;
- modular system architecture;
- functional integration;
- emergence.

MF4-A therefore asks only the ontology-level question:

> **What common relational structure, if any, survives across these cases?**

The goal is not yet to explain every mechanism of composition. It is to determine what must be distinguished before later rounds can study boundaries, hierarchy, time, multimodality, syntax, narrative and interaction.

---

# 2. First attack — plurality is not composition

Suppose a set contains:

`{red square, blue circle, green triangle}`.

The existence of three items supplies plurality.

It does not specify:

- adjacency;
- order;
- common boundary;
- binding;
- role;
- dependency;
- causal coupling;
- common task;
- temporal relation;
- hierarchy;
- interpretation.

Therefore:

`Plurality ≠ Composition`.

### Result

**CA-01 — Composition requires more than multiplicity/co-presence.**

---

# 3. Mereological composition is not yet organizational composition

Classical mereological questions ask when objects have a fusion/sum and what parthood means.

For Media Foundations this is insufficient.

Varzi's mereotopological analysis is directly useful: parthood and wholeness/connectedness are non-equivalent notions that can be combined in several ways rather than collapsing into one primitive.

A mereological sum can be defined over arbitrary scattered parts.

But a meaningful media/perceptual/representational composition generally needs additional organization.

### Result

**CA-02 — Mereological parthood/fusion and organizational composition are distinct. A collection can have a sum without forming a relevant organized whole.**

---

# 4. Physical contact is not universally necessary

A sentence's words may be spatially separated.

Network nodes may be physically distant.

A melody's notes do not coexist physically at one instant.

A distributed computation can combine states across machines.

A multimodal percept can relate sound and image from distinct transducers.

### Result

**CA-03 — Physical connectedness/contact is one possible compositional relation, not a universal requirement.**

---

# 5. Physical connectedness is not sufficient

Two objects glued together do not automatically form one functional, perceptual or representational composition for every question.

Palmer & Rock's uniform-connectedness work is important precisely because connected homogeneous regions can function as primitive perceptual units, but their own framework also requires depth/organization conditions: retinal connectedness alone does not guarantee one perceived unit.

### Result

**CA-04 — Connectedness can strongly support unit formation in some domains but does not universally establish composition.**

---

# 6. Grouping ≠ Composition

Grouping answers:

> Which elements are treated as belonging together?

Composition additionally asks:

> How are those members organized relative to one another and/or what larger structure do they jointly instantiate?

A bag of letters `{A,B,C}` is grouped.

The strings:

- ABC
- ACB
- BAC

use the same members but instantiate different ordered structures.

### Result

**CA-05 — Membership/grouping and internal organization are distinct.**

---

# 7. Binding ≠ Membership

Feature-integration experiments provide a hard falsifier.

If a display contains a red X and a green O, observers under attentional constraints can report illusory conjunctions such as a green X.

The relevant features were present, but the feature-to-object bindings were wrong.

Thus:

`Correct constituent inventory ≠ Correct composition`.

### Result

**CA-06 — Composition can fail through relational misbinding even when all constituent distinctions are present.**

---

# 8. Composition therefore needs a relation structure

The minimum candidate cannot be:

`Whole = set(parts)`.

It must be closer to:

`Composition = Components + Organization Relations`.

Let:

- `P = {p_1,...,p_n}` be candidate components;
- `R = {r_1,...,r_m}` be relations/constraints among component roles, states or boundaries;
- `C` be context/scope;
- `W` be the composed whole/profile if one is established.

Then a first structural skeleton is:

`Comp(P, R | C) -> W`.

### Result

**CA-07 — A composition attribution requires components plus typed organization relations/constraints, not components alone.**

---

# 9. Relation structure need not be pairwise

Some compositional constraints are not reducible to independent pairwise links.

Examples:

- chord quality depending on three or more tones;
- parity constraints;
- grammatical constructions with nested roles;
- global layout alignment;
- synchronization among many oscillators;
- ensemble balance;
- multi-agent protocol quorum.

### Result

**CA-08 — Compositional organization can be n-ary/global; a simple pairwise graph is not universally sufficient.**

---

# 10. Composition is scope-relative without becoming arbitrary

Whether two objects form one composition depends on the question/domain.

Two icons may be:

- separate UI controls at interaction level;
- one toolbar at layout level;
- part of one application screen at document/interface level.

This does not mean any grouping is equally valid.

Each level must be supported by actual relations/constraints relevant to that scope.

### Result

**CA-09 — Composition is scale/scope-relative but constrained by real organization relations.**

---

# 11. Composition can be nested

A letter composes a word.

Words compose a phrase.

Phrases compose a sentence.

Sentences compose a paragraph.

But composition need not always form a strict tree.

One component can participate in overlapping organizations.

### Result

**CA-10 — Recursive/nested composition is common but strict tree hierarchy is not universal.**

---

# 12. Overlapping composition

A musician can belong simultaneously to:

- rhythm section;
- ensemble;
- stage-left spatial cluster;
- harmonic voice-leading relation.

A UI element can be part of:

- a layout group;
- a form-validation dependency graph;
- a keyboard-navigation order;
- an accessibility tree.

### Result

**CA-11 — Components may participate in multiple overlapping compositional structures under different relation types/scopes.**

---

# 13. Hierarchy ≠ Composition

Simon famously emphasized hierarchical and near-decomposable organization in complex systems.

This is a powerful recurring architecture because strong within-subsystem interactions and weaker between-subsystem interactions can make complexity tractable.

But composition can also be:

- flat;
- cyclic;
- heterarchical;
- mesh-like;
- recurrent;
- overlapping.

### Result

**CA-12 — Hierarchy/near-decomposability is an important composition profile, not the definition of composition.**

---

# 14. Order can be constitutive

Consider:

- `dog bites man`
- `man bites dog`.

Same lexical members, different relation/order, different whole content.

Similarly:

- frames reordered in montage;
- instructions reordered in program execution;
- musical notes reordered in melody;
- events reordered in narrative.

### Result

**CA-13 — In ordered compositions, permutation can change whole-level identity/content while constituent identity remains fixed.**

---

# 15. But order is not universally required

A set-like composition, unordered chord-class abstraction or simultaneous field may not privilege sequence order.

### Result

**CA-14 — Order is a typed compositional relation, not a universal primitive.**

---

# 16. Spatial layout is more than membership

A page containing identical words can produce different reading structure depending on:

- position;
- columns;
- indentation;
- alignment;
- proximity;
- enclosure;
- whitespace;
- figure-ground organization.

### Result

**CA-15 — Spatial relations can be content-/function-bearing compositional structure.**

---

# 17. Temporal composition is more than succession

Events A then B are not automatically one event/composition.

A temporal whole may require some combination of:

- continuity;
- causal dependency;
- common goal;
- common actor;
- predictive regime;
- rhythm;
- bounded episode;
- narrative relation.

### Result

**CA-16 — Temporal succession is not sufficient for temporal composition.**

---

# 18. Simultaneity is not integration

Audio and video arriving at the same time are co-present.

They may still be:

- unrelated;
- misaligned;
- perceived as separate sources;
- transported in one container but semantically independent.

### Result

**CA-17 — Co-occurrence/synchrony can support composition but is neither universally necessary nor sufficient.**

---

# 19. Multiplexing ≠ Binding

A network packet/container can carry several channels together.

That is transport-level composition/multiplexing.

It does not imply the receiver perceptually or semantically binds the channels into one object/event.

### Result

**CA-18 — Signal multiplexing, perceptual binding and representational composition are distinct composition modes.**

This is a major MF4 non-collapse.

---

# 20. Aggregation ≠ Integration

Aggregation can collect component outputs without making them mutually constrain one another.

Integration implies some stronger coordinated combination, interaction or common-state dependence.

Example:

- dashboard listing independent metrics = aggregation/layout composition;
- fused estimator combining them into one state estimate = computational integration.

### Result

**CA-19 — Aggregation and integration are distinct strengths/modes of composition.**

---

# 21. Interaction is not universally required

Printed words in a sentence need not dynamically interact after printing.

Their standing composition is stabilized by syntax/layout/convention.

Thus composition can be static/dispositional.

### Result

**CA-20 — Current causal interaction among parts is not universally required for compositional standing.**

This mirrors MF3's standing-vs-active distinction.

---

# 22. Dynamic composition

Other wholes exist only through ongoing interaction:

- synchronized ensemble;
- flocking pattern;
- active control loop;
- conversation;
- multiplayer coordination;
- recurrent neural coalition.

### Result

**CA-21 — Composition can be standing/static or dynamically sustained.**

---

# 23. Composition can be stateful

The same current components/relations may have different whole meaning depending on history.

Examples:

- callback in conversation;
- motif reprise in music;
- state machine;
- undo stack;
- narrative foreshadowing/payoff.

### Result

**CA-22 — Compositional structure can depend on history/state, not only instantaneous relations.**

---

# 24. Role matters independently of part identity

The same part can occupy different roles.

`LOVES(A,B)` vs `LOVES(B,A)` already showed this in MF3-E.

Composition generalizes the lesson beyond symbols.

A camera angle can function as:

- establishing shot;
- reaction shot;
- insert;
- transition.

### Result

**CA-23 — Component identity and compositional role are distinct.**

---

# 25. Replaceability is typed

Some compositions tolerate component substitution while preserving whole identity/function.

Others do not.

Examples:

- replace a server replica;
- replace one violinist;
- replace one exact historical manuscript page;
- replace a key plot event.

### Result

**CA-24 — Component substitutability is composition-/role-/scope-relative, not a universal criterion of part identity.**

---

# 26. Compositional invariance

A whole may remain equivalent under certain transformations:

- translation/rotation of layout;
- transposition of melody;
- alpha-renaming variables;
- swapping redundant replicas;
- lossless file re-encoding.

### Result

**CA-25 — Composition identity requires an explicit invariance/equivalence class; physical arrangement identity is not universally required.**

---

# 27. Composition failure taxonomy begins here

Distinct failures include:

## Omission

Required component missing.

## Intrusion

Extraneous component included.

## Misbinding

Correct parts assigned to wrong roles.

## Misordering

Correct parts in wrong sequence.

## Misalignment

Spatial/temporal/register relations wrong.

## Constraint violation

Relations violate composition rules.

## Boundary error

Wrong units grouped/split.

## Synchronization error

Dynamic components fail temporal coordination.

## Interface mismatch

Parts cannot exchange/use compatible structures.

### Result

**CA-26 — Composition failure is not one scalar defect; error types map to different organizational relations.**

---

# 28. Whole-level properties need careful treatment

The slogan `whole is more than sum of parts` is too vague for foundations.

A whole-level property may arise because:

- relation/order changes behavior;
- constraints reduce the joint state space;
- interaction creates feedback;
- grouping changes perceptual access;
- composition creates new affordances;
- an evaluator/consumer operates only on the whole.

### Result

**CA-27 — Whole-level novelty should be explained through organization/constraints/interactions/consumer relations rather than treated as unexplained holism.**

---

# 29. Emergence ≠ Composition

Some compositions have no surprising emergent property.

A tuple `(x,y)` is still a composition.

Emergence can occur in nontrivial dynamical systems but is not required for composition.

### Result

**CA-28 — Emergence is a possible whole-level consequence of composition, not a constitutive condition.**

---

# 30. Compositional constraint reduces possibility space

Without relational constraints, component states may combine freely.

Composition can restrict admissible joint states.

Let component state space be:

`S = S_1 × ... × S_n`.

A composition can impose constraint set:

`Ω ⊂ S`.

Examples:

- grammar excludes malformed sequences;
- mechanical joints constrain relative motion;
- synchronization constrains phase;
- protocol constrains message order;
- visual binding constrains feature ownership.

### Result

**CA-29 — A useful general view of composition is structured restriction/organization of joint possibilities, not merely creation of a set.**

---

# 31. Constraint alone is not sufficient

Two unrelated variables can be statistically constrained by an external coincidence.

A composition requires that the relevant constraint relation participate in establishing the whole/organization under the declared scope.

### Result

**CA-30 — Constraint is a core descriptive tool but arbitrary statistical dependence is not sufficient for composition.**

---

# 32. Composition can create new operations

Once parts are organized, consumers/systems may operate on the whole:

- read a sentence;
- navigate a map;
- drag a grouped object;
- call a software module;
- recognize a chord;
- reason over a scene.

These operations may not be well-defined over isolated parts.

### Result

**CA-31 — Whole-level operational addressability is a strong composition capability, though not required for every minimal standing composition.**

---

# 33. Composition can change causal powers

Assembled components can enable effects unavailable separately:

- circuit closes;
- key + lock opens;
- ensemble forms control loop;
- chemical structure acquires different reactivity;
- UI form submission validates across fields.

### Result

**CA-32 — Composition can create relational causal powers without requiring mysterious nonphysical emergence.**

---

# 34. Composition can change representational content

MF3-E already demonstrated this for syntax.

MF4 generalizes:

`Content(whole)` need not equal unordered sum of `Content(parts)`.

Whole content can depend on:

- order;
- binding;
- layout;
- hierarchy;
- juxtaposition;
- temporal relation;
- context.

### Result

**CA-33 — Representational composition can contribute relational content not carried by isolated components.**

---

# 35. But composition need not be representational

A physical gear train is composition even if no part represents another.

A signal multiplexing scheme can be compositional without semantic content.

### Result

**CA-34 — Composition is broader than representational composition.**

---

# 36. Perceptual grouping need not equal physical grouping

Palmer & Rock show perceived organization depends on relations in the represented scene, not merely raw retinal adjacency/connectedness.

Likewise occluded fragments can be perceptually grouped into one object.

### Result

**CA-35 — Perceptual composition is evidence-/organization-relative and can diverge from physical connectedness.**

---

# 37. Binding problem is one composition problem, not the universal problem

Treisman & Gelade's feature-integration theory and illusory-conjunction experiments show a specific visual feature-binding problem.

Composition additionally includes:

- temporal ordering;
- semantic relation;
- multi-agent coordination;
- structural assembly;
- model hierarchy.

### Result

**CA-36 — Feature binding is a special case of composition, not its definition.**

---

# 38. Composition boundaries can be uncertain

A perceptual scene may admit ambiguous grouping.

A musical phrase boundary may be graded.

A narrative event may span several subevents.

A software component boundary may be architectural rather than physical.

### Result

**CA-37 — Composition can be uncertain/multi-scale; crisp boundaries are not universally constitutive.**

---

# 39. Composition can be observer/system-relative without being arbitrary

Different systems may compose the same substrate differently because they exploit different relations.

Examples:

- visual system groups by surface;
- OCR groups marks into characters/words;
- layout engine groups boxes;
- screen reader uses accessibility tree.

Each composition is constrained by an actual consumer/task relation.

### Result

**CA-38 — Composition attribution can be system/task-relative while remaining objectively constrained by exploited relations and outcomes.**

---

# 40. Composition can be designed, learned, emergent or imposed

Possible origins:

- engineered assembly;
- learned perceptual grouping;
- public convention;
- self-organization;
- evolutionary organization;
- analyst-defined grouping.

Analyst-defined grouping becomes a system composition claim only if it tracks real relations relevant to the system/question.

### Result

**CA-39 — Composition provenance and composition mechanism are distinct from the composition relation itself.**

---

# 41. Composition standing vs active composition

MF3's standing/active distinction transfers usefully but not identically.

## Standing composition

Relations/constraints defining the whole are stably instantiated or specified even when no process is currently traversing them.

Examples:

- printed sentence;
- saved document layout;
- circuit diagram;
- stored graph;
- software dependency specification.

## Active composition

Components currently interact, synchronize, bind, execute or are jointly consumed.

Examples:

- orchestra performance;
- active UI interaction;
- running distributed system;
- online multisensory binding.

### Result

**CA-40 — Composition can have standing and active forms; ongoing interaction is only required for dynamically sustained compositions.**

---

# 42. Composition token/type/profile distinctions

A musical score specifies a composition type/structure.

A performance is a realization/token.

A recording is another artifact token.

The same composition can survive variation among performances.

### Result

**CA-41 — Composition specification/type, physical realization/token and active performance/episode are distinct identity levels.**

---

# 43. Relation ownership matters

A relation can be externally imposed by an analyst without being part of the composed system.

Example:

- two unrelated files happen to have equal byte length.

This equality is real but normally irrelevant to their composition.

### Result

**CA-42 — Not every relation among parts is composition-defining; the relation must be constitutive/recruited/constraining for the relevant whole/scope.**

---

# 44. Whole identity can survive part turnover

A team, conversation, distributed service or biological system can preserve composition while individual members change.

This requires continuity in:

- roles;
- constraints;
- interfaces;
- organization;
- history;
- functional pattern.

### Result

**CA-43 — Whole identity need not require fixed component-token identity.**

---

# 45. Part identity can depend on the whole

In some systems a component is individuated partly by its role in the whole:

- subject position in a sentence;
- node in a graph schema;
- movement in a musical phrase;
- actor role in an event.

### Result

**CA-44 — Component individuation and composition can be mutually constraining; parts are not always ontologically prior to wholes.**

This is why MF4-B must study segmentation/boundaries before assuming fixed atoms.

---

# 46. Composition is not necessarily bottom-up

A global schema/template/task can determine how local elements are interpreted/grouped.

Examples:

- sentence grammar constraining lexical role;
- scene context constraining object grouping;
- interface template assigning component roles;
- musical meter constraining note function.

### Result

**CA-45 — Composition can involve bottom-up, top-down and reciprocal constraints; `parts first, whole later` is not universal.**

---

# 47. Composition is not necessarily lossless

Integration can deliberately collapse distinctions:

- averaging sensors;
- downmixing audio;
- summarizing a document;
- grouping individual points into a cluster.

### Result

**CA-46 — Composition can preserve, transform or discard component distinctions; composition is not equivalent to reversible concatenation.**

---

# 48. Decomposition is not always the inverse of composition

If composition is lossy or creates history-dependent organization, decomposing the whole may not recover the original components/states.

### Result

**CA-47 — Composition and decomposition are distinct operations; invertibility is a typed property, not a universal assumption.**

---

# 49. Composition can create context for its parts

A word's interpretation can depend on the sentence.

A note's function can depend on chord/key.

A shot's meaning can depend on montage context.

### Result

**CA-48 — Whole context can modulate part function/content; composition is not always a one-way map from independently fixed parts to whole.**

---

# 50. Local and global coherence are distinct

A structure can be locally well-formed but globally incoherent.

Examples:

- grammatically valid sentences forming contradictory paragraph;
- locally aligned video/audio segments with global narrative mismatch;
- software modules passing unit tests but failing integration.

### Result

**CA-49 — Composition can have multiple coherence scales; local validity does not guarantee global coherence.**

---

# 51. Composition can be partial

A set of components may be partially organized:

- some features correctly bound, others ambiguous;
- some services integrated, others loosely coupled;
- some narrative events causally linked, others merely adjacent.

### Result

**CA-50 — Composition strength/completeness can be graded/profiled; there need not be one binary threshold for every domain.**

---

# 52. Composition vs collection vs assembly vs integration

MF4-A provisional vocabulary:

## Collection

Membership relation only or primarily.

## Aggregation

Collection plus some common container/readout/grouping, with weak internal mutual constraint.

## Assembly

Parts occupy defined structural/functional relations in a larger mechanism/artifact.

## Binding

Distinct features/components are assigned to a common unit/role/object/event relation.

## Ordering

Relative sequence/precedence is composition-bearing.

## Integration

Component information/state/functions are combined into a common state/process/decision under mutual/joint constraints.

## Coordination

Components maintain compatible dynamic relations without necessarily merging states.

## Composition (umbrella)

Any grounded/relevant organization in which multiple components/roles/distinctions jointly instantiate a whole-level structure through typed relations/constraints.

### Result

**CA-51 — Composition is an umbrella relation family; collection, aggregation, assembly, binding, ordering, integration and coordination are distinct profiles.**

---

# 53. Minimal candidate definition

> **Composition is a relational organization in which a plurality of components, distinctions or roles jointly instantiate a whole-level structure because typed relations/constraints among them are constitutive or operationally relevant to that whole under a declared scope.**

Compact:

`Composition = Plurality + Constitutive Organization Relations + Whole/Scope`.

This remains provisional.

---

# 54. Why `whole-level structure` is needed

Without a whole/scope criterion, every real relation among any two objects could be called composition.

The whole criterion says the selected relations support at least one of:

- unit identity;
- common operation;
- common evaluation;
- bounded organization;
- system-consumer addressability;
- causal capability;
- representational content;
- coordinated dynamics;
- structural specification.

### Result

**CA-52 — Composition attribution requires a justified whole-level organization criterion, not merely arbitrary relation selection.**

---

# 55. Whole need not be a new physical object

A sentence whole, event whole, melody whole or protocol whole may be a relational pattern rather than a separately bounded material object.

### Result

**CA-53 — Composed wholes may be relational/processual/abstract structures, not only physical objects.**

---

# 56. Whole can be transient

A chord, perceptual group or conversational turn structure may last milliseconds/seconds.

### Result

**CA-54 — Persistence is not required for composition.**

---

# 57. Whole can be standing without active realization

A musical score can specify relations constituting a composition type while no performance is underway.

A program specifies call/dependency composition while not running.

### Result

**CA-55 — Specification-level composition and execution-level composition are distinct.**

---

# 58. Composition and representation do not share identical grounding

Representation needs standing-in/content grounding.

Composition needs organization relations that are constitutive/relevant to a whole.

A physical assembly can compose without representing.

### Result

**CA-56 — Composition grounding/relevance and representation grounding/content are distinct relation families.**

---

# 59. Composition and medium

A medium can support multiple simultaneous compositional structures:

- spatial layout;
- channels;
- temporal tracks;
- layers;
- linked documents.

The medium constrains possible relations but does not uniquely determine composition.

### Result

**CA-57 — Medium affords/constrains composition without uniquely specifying the resulting whole.**

---

# 60. Composition and signal

Signals can be composed through:

- multiplexing;
- mixing;
- coding blocks;
- packetization;
- synchronization;
- filtering networks.

These are signal-level compositions.

They must not be confused with perceptual/semantic integration.

### Result

**CA-58 — Signal composition concerns organization/transformation of active variation; higher-level perceptual/representational composition requires additional relations.**

---

# 61. Composition and perception

Perception actively groups/binds/segments evidence into units/events/fields/relations.

Palmer & Rock's uniform connectedness and Treisman's feature-binding work show that unit formation and binding are separate problems.

### Result

**CA-59 — Perceptual composition includes both unit formation and relation/binding among units; these must not be collapsed.**

---

# 62. Composition and representation

Representations can compose through:

- syntax;
- diagram structure;
- spatial layout;
- model coupling;
- narrative ordering;
- data schemas;
- reference/binding.

Whole content may depend on composition relations.

### Result

**CA-60 — Representational composition is composition whose organization contributes to content/use/evaluation under MF3 grounding.**

---

# 63. Composition and action/interaction

Interactive systems can compose:

- user actions;
- system responses;
- persistent state;
- constraints;
- feedback loops.

This yields action trajectories not reducible to a static layout.

### Result

**CA-61 — Interactive composition is a dynamic relation profile over agents/actions/states, not merely visual composition.**

---

# 64. Composition and narrative

Narrative is not simply a list of events.

At minimum it may recruit:

- temporal order;
- causal implication;
- perspective;
- salience;
- recurrence;
- expectation;
- framing.

MF4-A does not yet define narrative, but establishes that event membership alone cannot constitute narrative composition.

### Result

**CA-62 — Narrative composition is a strengthened temporal/representational profile, not event aggregation.**

---

# 65. Composition and montage

Two shots juxtaposed can produce an interpretation not recoverable from either shot alone because sequence/context changes relational content.

This is not mysterious emergence; it is relational content contributed by juxtaposition/order and consumer inference.

### Result

**CA-63 — Juxtaposition/order can be an active representational operation that alters whole interpretation while component images remain unchanged.**

---

# 66. Composition and music

A melody/chord/rhythm provides hard cases because:

- components can be transient;
- temporal relations matter;
- simultaneous and sequential relations coexist;
- transposition can preserve structural identity;
- whole function can alter note role.

### Result

**CA-64 — Composition ontology must support transient, relational and transformation-invariant wholes.**

---

# 67. Composition and software

Software clarifies multiple relation layers:

- containment;
- import/dependency;
- call graph;
- data flow;
- control flow;
- interface contract;
- deployment topology.

A single codebase can instantiate several distinct compositions simultaneously.

### Result

**CA-65 — One component set can support multiple orthogonal composition graphs; `the architecture` is often a typed family of relations.**

---

# 68. Composition and distributed systems

Simon-style near-decomposability becomes especially relevant:

- modules may have dense internal interaction;
- sparse/slower external interaction;
- hierarchy can reduce coordination complexity.

But strongly coupled peer networks remain compositions too.

### Result

**CA-66 — Coupling density/timescale can characterize composition modularity without defining composition itself.**

---

# 69. Composition and scenes

A scene can organize:

- objects;
- surfaces;
- spatial relations;
- events;
- agents;
- affordances;
- background fields.

Objects are not the universal unit.

### Result

**CA-67 — Scene composition must admit heterogeneous component types and relations, not only object collections.**

---

# 70. Homogeneous vs heterogeneous composition

Composition can combine:

- homogeneous parts: pixels, notes, agents;
- heterogeneous parts: text + image + control + metadata.

### Result

**CA-68 — Component type homogeneity is not required.**

---

# 71. Cross-level composition

A caption can constrain interpretation of an image.

A UI label modifies the action semantics of a button.

A soundtrack changes scene interpretation.

These combine components from different media/representation modes.

### Result

**CA-69 — Cross-medium/cross-mode composition can establish relations between heterogeneous representational systems.**

---

# 72. Composition interfaces

Whenever components retain partial autonomy, interfaces mediate their composition.

Interfaces may specify:

- accepted states/messages;
- timing;
- units;
- schemas;
- roles;
- error handling;
- ownership.

### Result

**CA-70 — Interface constraints are a major composition mechanism for modular/heterogeneous systems, but interface-mediated composition is one profile rather than the universal case.**

---

# 73. Composition can include negative constraints

What must **not** occur can be compositional structure:

- mutual exclusion;
- spacing;
- collision avoidance;
- grammar prohibition;
- forbidden transitions;
- masking.

### Result

**CA-71 — Composition relations include exclusions/constraints, not only positive links.**

---

# 74. Composition can include latent relations

A document may encode hyperlinks not currently traversed.

A score encodes timing relationships not currently performed.

### Result

**CA-72 — Composition relations can have standing/dispositional status independently of active realization.**

---

# 75. Composition can be probabilistic

Grouping/binding/event segmentation may be uncertain.

Systems may maintain distributions over possible compositions.

### Result

**CA-73 — Composition need not be represented as one deterministic partition/graph; uncertainty over organization is admissible.**

---

# 76. Multiple valid compositions can coexist

Ambiguous figure:

- one perceptual grouping now;
- another grouping later.

Music:

- same notes analyzed under different harmonic functions.

Text:

- syntactic ambiguity.

### Result

**CA-74 — Composition need not be globally unique; competing organizations can be valid/hypothesized under different constraints/scopes.**

---

# 77. Composition attribution needs provenance/evidence separation

As in MF3, distinguish:

## Composition ontology

What organizational relations constitute the whole.

## Composition attribution evidence

Why we believe those relations are used/real/constitutive.

Evidence can include:

- behavioral grouping;
- perturbation;
- structural constraints;
- interface contracts;
- causal coupling;
- transformation invariance;
- consumer operations.

### Result

**CA-75 — Evidence for a composition and the composition relation itself are distinct.**

# 79. Provisional minimal definition

> **A composition is a scope-relative relational organization in which a plurality of components, distinctions or roles jointly instantiate a whole-level structure because selected relations, constraints, bindings, orderings or couplings among them are constitutive or operationally relevant to that whole.**

Compact:

`Composition = Plurality + Typed Constitutive Relations/Constraints + Whole-level Organization + Scope`.

# 80. Provisional composition strength/profile

## C0 — Collection

Membership only.

## C1 — Grouped aggregate

Common container/unitization but weak internal relation.

## C2 — Relational composition

Internal ordering/binding/layout/dependency relations matter.

## C3 — Integrated composition

Parts jointly determine a common state/output/operation.

## C4 — Dynamically coordinated composition

Whole depends on ongoing coupled dynamics/synchronization/feedback.

## C5 — Hierarchical/recursive composition

Compositions become reusable parts of larger compositions.

## C6 — Cross-modal/cross-system composition

Heterogeneous media/representations/interfaces are coordinated under shared whole-level constraints.

These are profiles, not a mandatory scalar ladder.

# 83. Primary/original literature anchors

- Varzi, A. C. (1996), `Parts, Wholes, and Part-Whole Relations: The Prospects of Mereotopology`, *Data & Knowledge Engineering* 20(3), 259–286. DOI: 10.1016/S0169-023X(96)00017-1. Separates mereological parthood from topological wholeness/connectedness and compares several formal integration strategies.
- Treisman, A. M. & Gelade, G. (1980), `A Feature-Integration Theory of Attention`, *Cognitive Psychology* 12(1), 97–136. DOI: 10.1016/0010-0285(80)90005-5. Classic feature/conjunction distinction and attention-linked binding account.
- Treisman, A. & Schmidt, H. (1982), `Illusory Conjunctions in the Perception of Objects`, *Cognitive Psychology* 14(1), 107–141. DOI: 10.1016/0010-0285(82)90006-8. Demonstrates incorrect recombination of present features, a direct empirical falsifier of constituent-inventory = correct composition.
- Palmer, S. E. & Rock, I. (1994), `Rethinking Perceptual Organization: The Role of Uniform Connectedness`, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 1(1), 29–55. DOI: 10.3758/BF03200760. Proposes uniform connectedness as an entry-level unit-formation principle while distinguishing unit formation, grouping and parsing and noting depth-dependent exceptions.
- Simon, H. A. (1962), `The Architecture of Complexity`, *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society* 106(6), 467–482. Introduces hierarchy and near decomposability as recurring organization principles in complex systems; MF4 retains these as strong architecture profiles rather than universal composition criteria.

# 85. The most important structural reconstruction

Before MF4-A, one tempting picture was:

```text
Parts → combine → Whole
```

This is too linear.

The stronger candidate is reciprocal:

```text
Candidate distinctions/material
          │
          ├── segmentation / unitization
          ▼
        Parts/Roles
          │  ▲
          │  │ whole/context can constrain part identity/role
          ▼  │
   Relations / Constraints
          │
          ▼
   Whole-level organization
          │
          ├── operations / causal powers
          ├── perceptual grouping/binding
          ├── representational content
          ├── dynamic coordination
          └── evaluation/coherence
```

So **part individuation itself can depend on the proposed whole**.

This observation determines the next round.

# Final MF4-A handoff

The deepest result of MF4-A is simple but restrictive:

> **A composition is not a pile of parts. It is a plurality whose parts/roles jointly instantiate a whole because selected relations or constraints among them matter constitutively or operationally to that whole under a scope.**

This gives the provisional formula:

`Composition = Plurality + Typed Constitutive Relations/Constraints + Whole-level Organization + Scope`.

But it immediately exposes a new dependency:

> **Before asking how parts compose, we must know how parts and boundaries are constituted.**

Therefore MF4-A remains provisional and hands off directly to boundary/unit ontology.
